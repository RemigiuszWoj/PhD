\begin{abstract}
Evaluating a neighborhood on a gate-model quantum processor has so far been
confined to the smallest scheduling instances, because the swap-selection QUBO
needs one variable per candidate move and current devices hold a few dozen
qubits. We remove that ceiling with a windowed decomposition that applies to
all four neighborhoods used by the annealing line, including the one-hot
Adjacent encoding, for which solving one window per position and keeping the
best proposal computes the same argument of the minimum as the undecomposed
problem. Every window of a move is submitted as one job, subject to a
hundred-circuit cap that the provider documents nowhere but on submission.
The method reaches the entire Taillard benchmark on sixteen qubits, and we
report 223 runs covering all twelve configurations from twenty jobs to five
hundred, which is the first measurement of gate-model neighborhoods beyond
twenty. One neighborhood evaluation outlasts the longest budget of the
protocol, so we compare quality per move rather than per second. Deviation
from the lower bound tracks the ratio of machines to jobs rather than either
alone, and a paired comparison over 47 instances places the dense
neighborhoods ahead of the linear ones in 37 of them. Against a classical loop
on the same instances the margin falls from 8.7 to 1.1 as instances grow,
because the classical search runs out of budget rather than the quantum one
improving. We also report what the campaign cost, at 0.445 credits per
circuit, and what finishing it would have cost.
\keywords{Permutation flow shop \and QAOA \and Quantum neighborhoods
          \and Iterated local search \and Taillard benchmark}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The permutation flow shop problem remains strongly NP-hard for three or more
machines, and practical solvers evaluate neighborhoods millions of times inside
local search. Recent work replaces that evaluation with a quantum subroutine,
either on an annealer or on a gate processor, but every gate-model study so far
stops at twenty jobs because the swap-selection QUBO needs $K = n-1$ variables
for the linear neighborhoods and $O(n^2)$ for the dense ones, while current
devices hold sixteen to fifty-four qubits.

This paper removes that ceiling. A windowed decomposition carries all four
neighborhoods to the entire Taillard range on a sixteen-qubit processor, and the
measurements that follow are the first for gate-model neighborhoods beyond
$n = 20$. The results also show where the comparison protocol inherited from the
annealing line breaks down, since one neighborhood evaluation already costs more
wall-clock time than the longest budget under which the classical baselines were
measured.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
Scheduling $n$ jobs through $m$ machines in one shared order is strongly
NP-hard once $m$ reaches three~\cite{garey1976}, and the benchmark that
settled how such methods are compared is Taillard's~\cite{taillard1993}, whose
twelve configurations and lower bounds this paper uses throughout. Ruiz and
Maroto surveyed the heuristics built on it and found local search dominating
the field~\cite{ruizmaroto2005}, which puts the weight on what a single move is
allowed to change. Congram, Potts and van de Velde widened that move into
dynasearch, where a dynamic program selects several independent interchanges at
once~\cite{congram2002}; Ahuja and colleagues placed such constructions in the
general setting of very large-scale neighborhoods, searched implicitly rather
than enumerated~\cite{ahuja2002}. The Motzkin neighborhood takes its arc
structure from the paths Aigner counts~\cite{aigner1998}, and is the densest of
the four studied here.

A move set that large is only tractable because most of it can be discarded
before it is examined. Nowicki and Smutnicki showed that a swap outside the
critical blocks cannot improve the makespan~\cite{nowicki1996}, a property
Smutnicki later placed on firmer worst-case ground~\cite{smutnicki1998}, and
Wodecki and Bo\.{z}ejko discard in addition every move whose delta is not
negative~\cite{wodecki04}. Both filters matter here for a reason they were not
designed for: together they decide how many candidate variables a quantum
device is asked to hold, and that count, not $n$, is what the decomposition of
Section~\ref{sec:windowed} has to fit.

Casting that selection as a quadratic binary program follows Lucas's catalogue
of Ising formulations~\cite{lucas2014ising}, and the machine that reads such a
matrix directly is the annealer of Kadowaki and
Nishimori~\cite{kadowaki1998}. Venturelli, Marchand and Rojo mapped whole job
shop instances onto one~\cite{venturelli2015}, which runs into the qubit budget
quickly, and Bo\.{z}ejko and colleagues instead handed the annealer the
neighborhood rather than the schedule~\cite{bozejko2021}. Our own line follows
that second route: the four swap-selection matrices and their comparison on an
annealer~\cite{wojewodzki2026bpasts}, the Motzkin neighborhood in
detail~\cite{wojewodzki2026soco}, its composite counterpart under tabu search
and simulated annealing~\cite{wojewodzki2026icaisc}, the combinatorial account
of the Motzkin arcs~\cite{wojewodzki2025exit}, and the windowed decomposition
that carried the dense neighborhoods to $n = 500$ on D-Wave
hardware~\cite{wojewodzki2026windowed}.

The gate model reads the same matrix through QAOA~\cite{farhi2014qaoa}, whose
behaviour at shallow depth Zhou and colleagues analysed in
detail~\cite{zhou2020}. Tuning its angles per instance is what makes the
algorithm expensive inside a metaheuristic, and two results remove that cost:
Galda and colleagues showed the optimal angles transfer between instances of
one problem family~\cite{galda2021}, and Wurtz and Lykov gave angles that need
no training at all~\cite{wurtz2021fixed}. What remains is the hardware, whose
limits Preskill set out for this era~\cite{preskill2018} and which we met in a
form rarely reported: a cap on circuits per job, a charge proportional to
quantum processor time, and a move that outlasts the budget it was measured
under. The gate-model study closest to this one is our
own~\cite{wojewodzki2026gcmm}, and it stops at twenty jobs for exactly the
reason this paper removes.

\section{Problem and QUBO Formulation}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Permutation flow shop and the four neighborhoods}
\label{subsec:pfsp}
An instance gives $n$ jobs and $m$ machines, each job passing the machines in
the same order, and a solution is a permutation $\pi$ of the jobs whose
makespan $C_{\max}(\pi)$ is the moment the final job clears the final
machine. The four neighborhoods differ in how far a single move reaches along
$\pi$: Adjacent exchanges one pair of consecutive jobs, Fibonacci applies a set
of pairwise disjoint consecutive exchanges, and Dynasearch and Motzkin select
non-crossing intervals whose endpoints are exchanged. That reach is what later
fixes how wide a window may be, so we record it here rather than in the
decomposition.

For every candidate move the makespan change $\delta_k$ follows in constant
time from the Head and Tail profiles of the current permutation, so a whole
candidate set is priced in one pass. Two classical filters cut that set before
it ever reaches a solver: the block property of Smutnicki discards moves that
cannot touch the critical path, and the no-positive-improvement rule drops
moves with $\delta_k \ge 0$. Both matter for what follows, because they make
the number of surviving candidates depend on the instance rather than on $n$
alone, and that number is what a device has to hold.

\subsection{Swap-selection QUBO}
\label{subsec:qubo}
Let the neighborhood supply $K$ candidates and let $x_k \in \{0,1\}$ record
whether candidate $k$ is applied, for $k = 1, \dots, K$. Choosing a compatible
subset is the quadratic program
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{x} \in \{0,1\}^{K}} \mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T} Q \mathbf{x},
  \qquad Q_{kk} = \delta_k, \qquad
  Q_{kl} = \lambda \ \text{ for } 1 \le k < l \le K \text{ in conflict},
  \label{eq:qubo}
\end{equation}
with $Q$ upper triangular so that each conflicting pair is charged once, and
with $\lambda = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \lvert \delta_k \rvert + 1$ large enough that no
infeasible selection can beat a feasible one. Adjacent is the one exception:
there every pair conflicts, so the constraint becomes one-hot and the penalty
moves onto the diagonal as well, with $Q_{kk} = \delta_k - P$ and
$Q_{kl} = 2P$, where $P = 2\max_k \lvert \delta_k \rvert + 1$.

Which pairs are charged is the only thing that separates the four
neighborhoods, and it is also what decides whether the matrix survives being
cut down to a window. Fibonacci charges a pair only when the two exchanges
touch the same position, which makes $Q$ tridiagonal, and Adjacent charges
every pair. Both patterns depend on the candidates alone, so restricting them
to a subset of positions yields exactly the matrix the full problem would have
assigned to that subset. The interval neighborhoods charge a pair whenever two
intervals cross, which is a relation between candidates rather than positions,
and Section~\ref{sec:windowed} treats them separately for that reason.

\subsection{Fixed-angle QAOA}
\label{subsec:qaoa}
Substituting $x_k = (Z_k + 1)/2$ turns~\eqref{eq:qubo} into an Ising
Hamiltonian $H_C$ over $K$ spins, and QAOA prepares
$\lvert \psi \rangle = e^{-i\beta H_M} e^{-i\gamma H_C} H^{\otimes K}
\lvert 0 \rangle^{K}$ at depth $p = 1$, with the transverse field
$H_M = \sum_{k=1}^{K} X_k$ as mixer and $\gamma \in [0, \pi]$,
$\beta \in [0, \pi/2]$. The two angles are optimized once per neighborhood on
a small set of representative matrices and then frozen, which keeps the online
cost of a move at one submission instead of the hundreds of circuits a
variational loop would spend before committing to a single move.

A move samples the circuit and keeps whichever assignment scores lowest
against the original $Q$, rather than whichever one came up most often. The
distinction decides whether the method works at all on a noisy device: at
$p = 1$ the modal outcome is usually the all-zeros assignment, which applies no
swap, or a high-energy string with many bits set. Energy-based readout also
makes the windowed solver of the next section well defined, since each window
is sampled and reduced independently and the winner is whichever assignment its
own matrix scores lowest.

\subsection{Window geometry}
A window of $w$ consecutive positions yields $w-1$ variables for the
tridiagonal and one-hot matrices but $w(w-1)/2$ for the pairwise one, so one
window size cannot serve both classes. On sixteen qubits this means $w = 17$ for
the linear neighborhoods against $w = 6$ for the dense ones, and the window of
19 used by the annealing study fits no gate device, since it would need 171
variables.

\subsection{Tridiagonal and pairwise windows}
Fibonacci's QUBO couples only physically adjacent swaps, so the matrix of a
position window is exactly the global matrix restricted to that window and the
decomposition leaves the formulation unchanged. Swaps straddling a window
boundary are resolved by the same non-overlap validation the single-QUBO path
uses.

\subsection{One-hot windows}
The global one-hot QUBO selects the candidate of lowest delta, so solving one
one-hot QUBO per window and keeping the best proposal computes the same
$\arg\min$ and still applies exactly one swap. This matters because the D-Wave
enhanced variants never decomposed the linear neighborhoods; they relied on the
annealer holding around 190 logical variables, which no gate device does.

\subsection{Batching the windows of a move}
Every window of one move is submitted as a single job, so the queue and the job
overhead are paid once per move rather than once per window.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Benchmarks, metaheuristic and metrics}
We follow the protocol of the windowed annealing study so that the rows stay
comparable: Taillard instances with $n \in \{20, 50, 100, 200, 500\}$ and
$m \in \{5, 10, 20\}$, percentage relative deviation from the Taillard lower
bound, and a wall-clock budget of ten seconds. Each neighborhood drives the
iterated local search of Louren\c{c}o, Martin and St\"{u}tzle~\cite{lourenco2003},
which perturbs and re-optimises a single incumbent, on the \texttt{sirius}
processor, sixteen qubits in a star topology, with $p = 1$ and 4096 shots per
circuit.

\subsection{Deviations from the annealing protocol}
Coverage is four instances of every configuration except \texttt{tai500\_20},
which has three. The reason is price rather than principle: one instance of
that file submits 428 circuits against the 48 of all three twenty-job
configurations together, so the last instance it would have consumed bought
eleven instances elsewhere instead.

Three departures from the annealing protocol are deliberate. Only iterated
local search is run, because a single move from the identity permutation
cannot distinguish it from simulated annealing; one run replaces five seeds,
because repeated runs sample shot noise alone and the measured spread is 0.00
points for Adjacent and 0.03 for Fibonacci; and four instances replace ten,
because at 0.445 credits per circuit the remaining six would have cost about
3\,500 credits beyond the allocation.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Solution quality across the Taillard range}
Table~\ref{tab:rpd} reports every configuration of the benchmark, from twenty
jobs on five machines to five hundred on twenty, which is the first time
gate-model neighborhoods have been measured beyond $n = 20$. A dense
neighborhood takes ten of the twelve columns and Fibonacci the other two, while
Adjacent takes none and has the worst mean. That ordering follows from what a move is allowed to do:
Adjacent applies exactly one swap, the others apply tens.

Deviation falls as the instances grow. The hardest column is
$20\times20$, where even the best neighborhood leaves 42.8\%, and the easiest
is $100\times5$ at 11.2\%; holding $m = 20$ and raising $n$ walks
monotonically down from 42.8 to 15.6. What the rows track is the ratio $m/n$,
which correlates with the best value of each column at $0.89$ against $-0.45$ for
$n$ alone and $0.56$ for $m$ alone; Fig.~\ref{fig:ratio} separates the two. The lower bound explains the direction:
machines per job measures how much idle time a schedule carries, and one move
recovers a fixed amount of it, so the same move is worth less where there is
more to recover.

The move row accounts for the rest. The budget admits two or three moves at
$n = 20$ and exactly one from $n = 50$ upward, so the columns differ in problem
size and in how much search the same ten seconds bought.

\subsection{Which neighborhood wins, instance by instance}
Absolute deviation varies far more between instances than between
neighborhoods: on \texttt{tai50\_5} the four instances span 12.7 points
while the four neighborhoods sit within 0.6 of each other, so
comparing column means would say little. The four neighborhoods run on the same
instances, however, so instance difficulty is a shared term that cancels in a
paired comparison, and we compare them pair by pair over the 47 combinations of
file and instance.

Against Adjacent, Fibonacci gains 1.39 points and wins 39 of the 47 pairs,
Motzkin gains 1.50 and wins 37, and Dynasearch gains 1.86 and wins 41; a
two-sided sign test puts all three below $p=0.001$. Grouped, the two dense
neighborhoods beat the two linear ones in 37 of 47 pairs ($p=0.0001$).
Counting outright winners, Motzkin takes nineteen pairs, Dynasearch eighteen,
Fibonacci seven and Adjacent three. Fig.~\ref{fig:paired} shows the three
distributions. The ranking is settled even though no column of
Table~\ref{tab:rpd} separates the four rows by more than the spread between its
own instances.

\subsection{Comparison with the classical baseline}
The same loop, the same instances and the same ten-second budget run entirely
on a CPU give Table~\ref{tab:cls}. Averaged over the four neighborhoods, the
classical search is ahead everywhere, which is the expected result and not the
interesting one.

What the column of ratios shows is that the margin closes as the instances
grow, from 8.7 times on \texttt{tai50\_5} to 1.1 on \texttt{tai500\_20}. The
reason is not that the gate pipeline improves with $n$: its own figures move
from 14.5\% to 15.9\%, which is flat. The classical baseline degrades instead,
from 1.7\% to 13.9\%, because ten seconds no longer suffice for it to converge
once the instance is large. Both methods are then far from the lower bound, and
they are far from it for different reasons -- one has run out of time, the
other has made a single move.

We draw no advantage from this. A gate processor that needs 1.48 seconds per
circuit cannot compete with a loop that completes thousands of iterations in
the same budget, and the narrowing margin measures the classical method
running out of budget rather than the quantum one catching up. It does say
where a future device would have to improve to matter: not in solution quality
per move, which is already within a factor of the classical move, but in how
many moves a second it can deliver.

\subsection{Collapse of the time-budget axis}

the neighborhood class, so the ten-second budget admits two moves at $n = 20$
and one from $n = 50$ upward. Every shorter budget of the protocol therefore
describes the same computation, which is why the comparison is reported per move
rather than per second.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The first move agrees across coupling maps}
Adjacent returns a makespan of 1390 on instance 0 of \texttt{tai20\_5} on
\texttt{garnet}, \texttt{emerald} and \texttt{sirius} alike, three different
coupling maps. The processors differ only in how many moves fit the budget, so a
cross-architecture comparison that does not hold the move count fixed measures
queue latency rather than fidelity.

\subsection{Non-additive deltas in the dense neighborhoods}
Fourteen of the 223 hardware runs returned the starting makespan, every one of
them in a dense neighborhood, and six of the eight Motzkin runs on
\texttt{tai20\_20} did so. The deltas entering the
QUBO are computed independently, while the pairwise penalties do not capture how
several swaps compose, so a set of individually improving swaps can worsen the
schedule; this limits the formulation rather than the device, and it applies to
the annealing line as well.

\subsection{What a campaign on this hardware costs}
\label{subsec:cost}
Three numbers govern the bill, and none of them is the one a reader would
guess. The provider charges for processor time at a published rate, 0.30
credits per second on the star processor against 0.50 and 0.75 on the two
lattice machines, and a job occupies the processor for 1.48 seconds per
circuit, flat from eight circuits to a hundred. A move therefore costs 0.445
credits for every window its decomposition produces, and nothing for the job
that carries them. Across the measurements reported here the model predicted
the balance to within half a percent of what the account was actually charged.

The consequence is that windowing, the very mechanism that makes large
instances reachable, is also what makes them expensive. One instance of
\texttt{tai500\_20} across the four neighborhoods submits 428 circuits and
costs 190 credits, while all three twenty-job configurations together submit 48
and cost 21. A ninefold price for a twelfth of the table.

A third limit is undocumented in the provider's pricing and appears only on
submission: a job may carry at most a hundred circuits. The dense
decomposition at $n = 500$ produces 166 windows and is refused outright, so
such moves must be split across jobs. Splitting is free, since the charge
follows circuits rather than submissions, but a framework that batches windows
without checking the count will have its job declined, receive empty
histograms, and -- unless it refuses them -- fall back to a classical move and
record the result as a quantum one.

Taken together these fix the price of the protocol the annealing line used. Ten
instances of each configuration, four neighborhoods, one run each, is 12\,260
circuits and about 5\,460 credits; repeating the dense neighborhoods five times
over, as a proper error bar would require, raises it to roughly 22\,000. We
report four instances because that is what a two-thousand-credit allocation
buys, 1\,992 of it spent across 223 runs, and we state the figure because no
previous study of quantum neighborhood evaluation reports what its campaign
cost or what finishing it would have cost.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}
A declined job returns an empty list of histograms without raising, and each
neighborhood then falls back to a classical greedy swap, so the run completes
and is stored as a hardware measurement while being entirely classical. We
detected this on a motzkin row whose moves took 2.5 seconds against the 12
seconds of the valid rows, and the guard now compares the number of returned
histograms against the number of submitted circuits.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
A windowed decomposition carries all four swap-selection neighborhoods to the
full Taillard benchmark on a sixteen-qubit processor, and the 223 runs reported
here are the first gate-model neighborhood measurements past twenty jobs. The
decomposition leaves the formulation intact: a window of the tridiagonal matrix
is the global matrix restricted to that window, and the one-hot case reduces to
the same argument of the minimum through a second selection over window
proposals. What the measurements settle is the ordering. Instance difficulty
swamps the differences between neighborhoods, but the four run on the same
instances, so a paired comparison removes it and places Dynasearch 1.86 points
ahead of Adjacent on 41 of 47 pairs, with the dense pair ahead of the linear
pair on 37.

What the measurements do not settle is whether any of this is worth doing on
present hardware, and the resource figures say why. A move costs 0.445 credits
for every window it produces and 1.48 seconds of processor time per circuit, so
one evaluation outlasts the longest budget under which the classical baselines
were measured, and the ten-instance protocol of the annealing line would cost
about 3\,500 credits beyond what we spent. The margin against a classical loop
narrows from 8.7 to 1.1 as instances grow, but it narrows because the classical
search exhausts its budget, not because the quantum one improves. The gap to
close is throughput rather than the quality of a single move, and a device that
delivered moves at classical rates would make the comparison worth repeating.
