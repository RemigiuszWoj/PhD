\begin{abstract}
\rev{The permutation flow shop problem (PFSP) is an NP-hard scheduling problem,
and local-search solvers for it spend most of their time evaluating
neighborhood moves. Earlier work moved this evaluation to quantum annealers by
writing each neighborhood as a quadratic unconstrained binary optimization
(QUBO) problem. This paper asks whether the same QUBOs can be solved on
gate-model processors. We embed the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm
(QAOA) in iterated local search (ILS) and simulated annealing (SA), reuse the
annealing QUBOs of four neighborhoods (Adjacent, Fibonacci, Dynasearch,
Motzkin), and fix the QAOA angles offline, so that each move costs one device
submission on noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) hardware. We compile the
four QUBOs onto four superconducting coupling maps and run them on three
processors: IBM Heron~r2, IQM Crystal~20 and IQM Star~16. The density of the
conflict graph sets the compiled depth, and the depth ordering of the
neighborhoods is the same on every coupling map, which matches the feasibility
ordering known from annealers. On ten Taillard $20\times5$ instances one QAOA
move lowers the mean relative deviation from the lower bound from 27.9\% to
between 22.1\% and 25.0\%. Short circuits return the same makespan on a
156-qubit and a 16-qubit device, instance by instance. Queueing stretches one
move to 10--288~s, so no run reached a second move within the 10~s budget, and
all quantum variants stay far behind classical neighborhood evaluation. The
results give a hardware-independent rule for choosing neighborhoods and a
measured cost per move.}
\keywords{combinatorial optimization, QUBO, metaheuristics, simulated
  annealing, circuit compilation on superconducting hardware, NISQ}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
The permutation flow shop problem (PFSP) is one of the most extensively
studied NP-hard problems in production scheduling. Given $n$ jobs that
must be processed in the same order on $m$ machines, the task is to find
a single job permutation that minimizes the makespan $C_{\max}$, the
completion time of the last job on the last machine. Because the number
of permutations grows factorially, exact methods are limited to small
instances, and practical solvers rely on local-search metaheuristics
such as iterated local search \rev{(ILS)}, simulated annealing \rev{(SA)}, and tabu search.
These methods spend the overwhelming majority of their running time in
one inner operation: evaluating the moves of a neighborhood to decide
where to step next. The cost of this evaluation, repeated millions of
times over a search, is the dominant computational bottleneck and the
natural target for acceleration.

A recent line of work offloads that evaluation to quantum hardware by
casting each neighborhood as a quadratic unconstrained binary
optimization (QUBO) problem and solving it on a quantum
annealer~\rev{\cite{bozejko2026fibonacci,wojewodzki2026windowed}}. The
annealer selects, in a single call, the subset of moves that jointly
improve the makespan without conflicting, and the classical
metaheuristic proceeds from the returned permutation. The gate-model
paradigm, however, has remained almost untouched for scheduling, even
though it offers programmable circuits, routing to arbitrary qubit
pairs, and steadily growing access through cloud platforms such as IBM
Quantum. Whether the same neighborhood-as-QUBO idea can be carried from
the annealer to a gate-model device, and what changes when it is, is the
question this paper addresses.

This paper introduces a gate-model framework that evaluates PFSP
neighborhoods with the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm
(QAOA)~\cite{farhi2014qaoa} embedded in classical iterated local search
and simulated annealing. The framework reuses the swap-selection QUBO
encoding of the annealing line, so that a single problem matrix drives
either an annealer or a QAOA circuit and the two paradigms are compared
on identical instances. To keep the method viable on noisy
intermediate-scale \rev{quantum (NISQ)} hardware, we adopt a fixed-angle QAOA whose
variational angles are pre-optimized offline and then frozen, which reduces
the online cost to one device submission per move, the same budget the
annealer spends. We formulate all four neighborhood structures used by
the annealing line and analyze how the density of their conflict graphs
governs circuit depth once the QUBO is compiled onto superconducting
hardware, on coupling maps ranging from a heavy-hex lattice to a
resonator-coupled star. Our central observation is that this depth
plays the role that embedding capacity plays on the annealer: it
produces the same feasibility ordering of the neighborhoods for a
different, compilation-grounded reason, and windowed decomposition tames
the dense neighborhoods just as it does on the annealer.

This work addresses the evaluation methodology and its analysis rather
than a large-scale hardware benchmark. Current gate-model devices are noisy
and shallow, so it matters to know in advance which neighborhoods compile to
shallow circuits, and cloud access is metered in minutes per month, which
makes the offline fixed-angle design necessary. Simulation enters
only once, offline, to fix the QAOA angles; every result we report is
measured on quantum processors.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:related}
The permutation flow shop problem has served as a testbed for scheduling
algorithms since Taillard released the benchmark instances that remain in
standard use today~\cite{taillard1993}. The problem is strongly NP-hard
once three or more machines are involved, as established by Garey,
Johnson, and Sethi~\cite{garey1976}, which is why practical solvers rely
on heuristics. Among constructive heuristics, the method of Nawaz,
Enscore, and Ham is still regarded as the strongest of its kind and is
the usual starting point for local search~\cite{neh1983}. Ruiz and Maroto
compared a large collection of heuristics and metaheuristics for the
makespan objective and confirmed that iterated local search and simulated
annealing are among the most effective approaches~\cite{ruizmaroto2005}.
These two metaheuristics, which host the quantum subroutine in our
framework, trace back to this literature: simulated annealing was
introduced as a general optimization method by Kirkpatrick, Gelatt, and
Vecchi~\cite{kirkpatrick1983}, and iterated local search was later
formalized by Louren\c{c}o, Martin, and St\"{u}tzle~\cite{lourenco2003}.
The power of such methods depends on how large a neighborhood they can
search at each step. Ahuja, Ergun, Orlin, and Punnen surveyed very
large-scale neighborhood search, in which a neighborhood of exponential
size is explored in polynomial time~\cite{ahuja2002}. Congram, Potts, and
van de Velde introduced dynasearch, which uses dynamic programming to
apply several independent interchange moves in a single step and gives
its name to the Dynasearch neighborhood studied here~\cite{congram2002}.

A more recent direction replaces the classical evaluation of these
neighborhoods with a quantum subroutine, encoding each neighborhood as a
QUBO whose solution selects a set of compatible improving swaps. \rev{Bo\.{z}ejko,
Uchro\'{n}ski, and Wodecki wrote the exponential Fibonacci neighborhood as a
QUBO and searched it on a D-Wave annealer inside a scheduling
metaheuristic~\cite{bozejko2026fibonacci}.} A companion paper
introduced the Motzkin neighborhood, whose non-crossing structure
produces a sparser conflict graph than the overlap-based
alternatives~\cite{wojewodzki2026soco}. A third made the approach
scalable through a windowed QUBO decomposition that keeps every
subproblem within the connectivity of the
annealer~\cite{wojewodzki2026windowed}. The present work adopts these
QUBO encodings without change and studies their behavior on gate-model
hardware. \rev{We take the QUBOs over unchanged on purpose, so that the solver
is the only thing that differs between the annealing and the gate-model runs;
the contribution of this paper lies in the gate-model pipeline, in the depth
analysis across coupling maps, and in the measurements on three processors.}

The mapping from combinatorial problems to quadratic binary form rests on
the Ising model, and Lucas cataloged Ising formulations for a broad
range of NP problems while bounding the number of spins each
requires~\cite{lucas2014ising}. Quantum annealing is the physical process
these formulations target; Kadowaki and Nishimori introduced it as a
transverse-field method for driving an Ising system toward its ground
state~\cite{kadowaki1998}. \rev{On real hardware, Carugno, Ferrari Dacrema, and Cremonesi evaluated
job-shop scheduling on a D-Wave annealer and reported that the embedding
restricts the size of the instances it can take~\cite{carugno2022}.}

The gate-model counterpart of quantum annealing for optimization is the
quantum approximate optimization algorithm of Farhi, Goldstone, and
Gutmann, which alternates cost and mixer layers whose angles are tuned to
minimize the objective~\cite{farhi2014qaoa}\rev{; Blekos et al.\ review the
variants that followed~\cite{blekos2024}}. Zhou, Wang, Choi, Pichler,
and Lukin analyzed its optimization landscape on near-term devices and
found that good angles follow a regular pattern rather than scattering
across the parameter space~\cite{zhou2020}. Wurtz and Lykov turned this
regularity into fixed-angle conjectures that supply near-optimal
parameters without instance-specific tuning~\cite{wurtz2021fixed}, and
Galda, Liu, Lykov, Alexeev, and Safro showed that optimal angles transfer
between graphs that share local structure~\cite{galda2021}. \rev{Sureshbabu et
al.\ extended such parameter rules to weighted problems, where the angles are
rescaled by the weights~\cite{sureshbabu2024}; our QUBOs are weighted in the
same sense.} Together
these results are what make the offline, fixed-angle design of our
framework possible. The closest gate-model work on scheduling is that of
Amaro, Rosenkranz, Fitzpatrick, Hirano, and Fiorentini, who ran several
variational quantum heuristics, QAOA among them, on a small job-shop
instance and found convergence on present hardware difficult~\cite{amaro2022}.
\rev{Kurowski et al.\ applied QAOA to the job shop problem with a full-problem
encoding and tested it on small instances~\cite{kurowski2023}, and
Bo\.{z}ejko et al.\ studied hybrid quantum approximate algorithms for the flow
shop problem~\cite{bozejko2024cinti}. Pelofske, B\"{a}rtschi, and Eidenbenz compared
short-depth QAOA circuits with quantum annealing on the same Ising models and
found the annealer ahead on current hardware~\cite{pelofske2024}.}
Our neighborhood-level encoding keeps each circuit small, which is
precisely what makes a fixed-angle approach practical where an
end-to-end formulation struggles.

The hardware itself determines which circuits can run at all. Preskill
characterized the noisy intermediate-scale quantum regime, in which noise
sharply limits the depth of a reliable circuit~\cite{preskill2018}\rev{, and
Kim et al.\ showed that circuits of a few thousand two-qubit gates on a
heavy-hex processor still give usable expectation values once error
mitigation is applied~\cite{kim2023}}. IBM's
superconducting processors are built on the heavy-hexagonal lattice of
Chamberland, Zhu, Yoder, Hertzberg, and Cross, which reduces frequency
collisions at the cost of sparse qubit connectivity~\cite{chamberland2020}.
Compiling a QUBO with many couplings onto such a lattice therefore
requires routing, and Li, Ding, and Xie gave an efficient qubit-mapping
and routing method whose inserted SWAP gates are exactly what deepen the
circuits analyzed here~\cite{li2019}. Circuit depth on this hardware thus
assumes the role that embedding capacity plays on the annealer, which is
the link this paper develops.

\section{Research Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

Our framework leaves the classical metaheuristic untouched and replaces
only the neighborhood-evaluation oracle with a gate-model quantum
subroutine. Every move evaluation is cast as a QUBO instance, mapped to
an Ising Hamiltonian, and solved approximately by a fixed-angle QAOA
circuit~\cite{farhi2014qaoa}\rev{, while the QUBOs and the ILS and SA hosts are
those of the annealing line~\cite{wojewodzki2026soco,wojewodzki2026windowed}}.

\subsection{Swap-selection QUBO encoding}
\label{subsec:qubo}
We reuse the swap-selection encoding of the annealing
line~\rev{\cite{wojewodzki2026windowed}}. The neighborhood supplies $K$ swap
candidates, and a binary variable $x_k=1$, for $k=1,\dots,K$, indicates
that candidate $k$ is applied to the current permutation. Selecting
a subset of compatible swaps is written as the minimization of a
quadratic form
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{K}}\
  \mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T} Q\,\mathbf{x},
  \label{eq:qubo}
\end{equation}
where $Q_{kk}=\delta_k$, for $k=1,\dots,K$, is the makespan change induced
by swap $k$, evaluated in $O(1)$ time through the Head--Tail decomposition.
The matrix is upper triangular, so for $1\le k<l\le K$ the entry $Q_{kl}$
equals $\lambda$ when candidates $k$ and $l$ conflict and zero otherwise, and
each conflicting pair is penalized once. The penalty
\begin{equation}
  \lambda \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{K}\lvert\delta_k\rvert \;+\; 1
  \label{eq:penalty}
\end{equation}
dominates the objective, so any solution that selects two conflicting
swaps is more expensive than any feasible one. The one-hot Adjacent encoding
is the exception. There every pair conflicts, so the penalty also enters the
diagonal, with $Q_{kk}=\delta_k-P$ and $Q_{kl}=2P$ for $k<l$, where
$P=2\max_{k}\lvert\delta_k\rvert+1$. The pattern of penalized
pairs is the fingerprint of the neighborhood
(Section~\ref{subsec:neigh}).

\subsection{From the QUBO to a QAOA circuit}
\label{subsec:circuit}
The change of variables $Z_k=2x_k-1$, i.e.\ $x_k=(1+Z_k)/2$, turns the
QUBO~\eqref{eq:qubo} into an Ising cost
Hamiltonian~\cite{lucas2014ising}
\begin{equation}
  H_C \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{K} h_i Z_i \;+\; \sum_{1\le i<j\le K} J_{ij} Z_i Z_j
  \;+\; \mathrm{const},
  \label{eq:ising}
\end{equation}
where $Z_i$ and $X_i$ are the Pauli-$Z$ and Pauli-$X$ operators acting on
qubit $i$, with one qubit per swap candidate, and the linear fields $h_i$ and
the couplings $J_{ij}$ follow directly from the entries of $Q$. A depth-$p$ QAOA prepares the parameterized
state
\begin{equation}
  \lvert\gamma,\beta\rangle \;=\;
  \Bigl(\textstyle\prod_{l=1}^{p}
        e^{-i\beta_l H_M}\,e^{-i\gamma_l H_C}\Bigr)
  \lvert +\rangle^{\otimes K},
  \qquad H_M=\sum_{i=1}^{K} X_i,
  \label{eq:qaoa}
\end{equation}
with the transverse-field mixer $H_M$. The circuit carries $p$ layers and two
angles per layer, $\gamma_l$ for the cost evolution and $\beta_l$ for the
mixer, which we optimize over $\gamma_l\in[0,\pi]$ and
$\beta_l\in[0,\pi/2]$. For $p=1$ each layer compiles to elementary gates. An initial layer of Hadamard gates places the register
in an equal superposition over all move selections. The cost evolution
$e^{-i\gamma H_C}$ is realized as a single-qubit rotation
$R_Z(2\gamma h_i)$ on every variable together with a two-qubit rotation
$R_{ZZ}(2\gamma J_{ij})$ on every coupled pair, and the mixer
$e^{-i\beta H_M}$ as a rotation $R_X(2\beta)$ on every qubit. \rev{Sampling
the circuit gives a set of bitstrings, each a candidate swap set;
Section~\ref{subsec:move} explains how one of them becomes the move.} Figure~\ref{fig:qaoa_fib}
shows the resulting $p=1$ circuit for a small Fibonacci instance. The set
of $R_{ZZ}$ gates is exactly the edge set of the conflict graph of $Q$,
so the neighborhood structure alone determines the two-qubit layer of the
circuit.

\subsection{Fixed-angle QAOA with offline angle optimization}
\label{subsec:fixed}
Variational QAOA re-optimizes the angles $(\gamma,\beta)$ for every
instance, spending tens to hundreds of circuit executions before a single
move is chosen. Under NISQ noise and a tight quantum-runtime budget---the
IBM Open plan grants roughly ten minutes of \rev{quantum processing unit (QPU)} time per twenty-eight-day
window---this is not viable inside a metaheuristic that evaluates
thousands of moves. We therefore fix the angles per neighborhood type and
obtain them offline, once. For each of the four neighborhoods a single
angle pair $(\gamma^\star,\beta^\star)$ is optimized on a classical
statevector simulation by an exhaustive \rev{grid of $25\times13$ points} over that
range, by minimizing the expected cost
\begin{equation}
  (\gamma^\star,\beta^\star)
  \;=\; \arg\min_{\gamma,\beta}\
  \langle\gamma,\beta\lvert H_C\rvert\gamma,\beta\rangle,
  \qquad \gamma\in[0,\pi],\ \beta\in[0,\pi/2],
  \label{eq:angleopt}
\end{equation}
averaged over a \rev{training set of windowed QUBOs of that neighborhood. The set
comes from three Taillard instances (tai20\_5 nos.~0 and~1, tai20\_10 no.~0),
each taken at the identity permutation and cut into windows of six positions
with step three, which gives 21 QUBOs for Adjacent and Fibonacci and 11 for
Dynasearch and Motzkin. Two of the training instances are also among the ten
test instances, and because every run starts from the identity, their first
moves meet the training windows; at $p=1$ the optimal angles are nearly constant
across instances, so we do not expect this to bias the results. The resulting
angles $(\gamma^\star,\beta^\star)$ are $(19\pi/24,7\pi/24)$ for Adjacent,
$(21\pi/24,5\pi/24)$ for Fibonacci, $(17\pi/24,\pi/12)$ for Dynasearch and
$(17\pi/24,\pi/8)$ for Motzkin.} The angles are then frozen and reused for every move
of that neighborhood at run time, so the offline cost is amortized over
the whole search and the online cost per move stays at a single
submission. This strategy relies on angle transferability: for a fixed
neighborhood the optimal $p=1$ angles are near-constant across instances,
a property this design relies on\rev{, and which the fixed-angle conjectures of
Wurtz and Lykov~\cite{wurtz2021fixed} support for regular graphs}. On hardware each move therefore costs one
submission, the direct gate-model analog of the annealer's single
call per move, which is the basis for a fair wall-clock comparison. For
Adjacent and Fibonacci that submission holds a single circuit; for the
windowed neighborhoods it holds one circuit per window. We
work throughout at $p=1$, the depth that current NISQ hardware supports
reliably. Whether greater depths pay off on hardware is a question for future
work: it requires many more circuit executions than a queued, quota-limited
account allows, and therefore better access to the device than we had here.

\rev{\subsection{From a sample to a move}
\label{subsec:move}
Each circuit is sampled with 4096 shots, and we keep the sampled bitstring of
lowest energy under the original QUBO~\eqref{eq:qubo}. The single most frequent
bitstring of a $p=1$ state is usually the all-zeros assignment or a
high-energy state with many ones, so it is a poor readout. The bits equal to
one name the selected swap candidates. For the dense neighborhoods the
permutation is cut into windows of six positions that overlap by half, all
windows of a move are sent as one job, and the swaps selected in all windows
are joined into one list. A greedy pass from left to right then restores
feasibility: a Dynasearch swap $(i,j)$ is kept if it does not overlap the last
kept swap, a Motzkin swap is kept if it neither crosses a kept swap nor shares
an endpoint with it, and Fibonacci keeps adjacent swaps that do not share a
position. The same pass drops a swap that two overlapping windows both
selected. For Adjacent each window proposes at most one swap and the move takes
the proposal with the smallest $\delta_k$, which is the minimizer of the global
one-hot QUBO. The kept swaps touch disjoint positions, so they are applied at
once, and the makespan of the new permutation is then computed from scratch in
$O(nm)$ time rather than from the sum of the $\delta_k$. If no swap survives the
repair, the method falls back to the best single improving swap; no move
reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} came from this fallback, since every
reported Fibonacci, Dynasearch and Motzkin move changes four or more positions.}

\subsection{Neighborhoods and their conflict graphs}
\label{subsec:neigh}
All four neighborhoods reuse their annealing
QUBOs~\rev{\cite{wojewodzki2026windowed,wojewodzki2026soco}} and differ only in
the coupling pattern of the $R_{ZZ}$ layer in~\eqref{eq:qaoa}, that is,
in the topology of the conflict graph. The Adjacent neighborhood selects
one of the $n-1$ adjacent transpositions under a one-hot constraint, so
every pair of variables conflicts and its conflict graph is complete. The
Fibonacci neighborhood penalizes only consecutive overlapping swaps and
so yields a tridiagonal chain, the sparsest of the four. The Dynasearch
and Motzkin neighborhoods both act on endpoint-swap intervals but apply
different conflict rules: Dynasearch penalizes every pair of overlapping
intervals, nested ones included, whereas Motzkin admits nested intervals
and penalizes only crossings and shared endpoints. Motzkin's conflict
graph is therefore a subgraph of Dynasearch's and is strictly sparser,
which is precisely the property that motivates the Motzkin
neighborhood~\cite{wojewodzki2026soco}. The Adjacent and Dynasearch
graphs are dense and compile to the deepest circuits, while Fibonacci's
tridiagonal chain stays shallowest; Motzkin sits between them, sparser than
Dynasearch but far denser than Fibonacci.
Figure~\ref{fig:neigh_circuits} shows example $p=1$ circuits for the
Adjacent, Dynasearch, and Motzkin neighborhoods; the Fibonacci circuit is
the one in Fig.~\ref{fig:qaoa_fib}. Each $R_{ZZ}$ gate corresponds to one
edge of the conflict graph, so the two-qubit gate count is read directly
off the graph.

\subsection{Compilation across coupling maps}
\label{subsec:depth}
An $R_{ZZ}$ gate requires its two qubits to be physically adjacent, so on any
fixed coupling map the compiler routes the remaining pairs through SWAP
networks, which deepen the circuit and inject additional noise. Circuit depth
after transpilation therefore plays the role that embedding capacity plays on
the annealer. To separate the effect of the coupling map from everything else,
we compiled the same four swap-selection QUBOs, taken from the first Taillard
$20\times5$ instance, onto four processors: the heavy-hex lattice of IBM
Heron~r2, the square lattices of IQM Crystal~20 and Crystal~54, and the star
architecture of IQM Star~16, where every qubit couples to one central
resonator and a \rev{qubit-resonator} MOVE operation takes the place of a SWAP network. All
figures are taken at $p=1$, with windows of six for the dense neighborhoods,
and summed over the circuits of one move; on Star~16 the two-qubit count is \rev{controlled-Z (CZ)}
plus MOVE. Transpilation consumes no device time, so this comparison cost no quota.

Table~\ref{tab:depth} admits two readings. The ordering of the four
neighborhoods by compiled depth is the same on every coupling map, with
Fibonacci well below Adjacent and both well below Motzkin and Dynasearch, so
the feasibility ordering the annealing line observed does not depend on the
choice of hardware. The absolute depths do depend on it, and heavy-hex is the
least favourable of the four, since the square lattices compile the same
circuits between 1.7 and 2.6 times shallower.

The Fibonacci row is the control. Its tridiagonal QUBO compiles to fourteen CZ
gates on all four maps, because a tridiagonal interaction graph embeds in a
path and needs no routing anywhere, so nothing in that row can be attributed
to the device. The star architecture removes routing for the dense
neighborhoods too, cutting the CZ count of Dynasearch from 1668 to 696, but it
pays in MOVE operations that serialize through the single resonator, which is
why its depth lands between the two lattices rather than below them.
Connectivity buys gate count, not depth.

\subsection{Hardware and software}
\label{subsec:hw}
The circuits target the IBM Heron~r2 processor \texttt{ibm\_fez} (156
qubits, heavy-hex coupling map with 352 couplings, native gate set CZ, RZ,
SX, X), accessed through Qiskit Runtime (\texttt{qiskit}~2.5.1 with
\texttt{qiskit-ibm-runtime}). A second set of measurements runs on two IQM
processors reached through IQM Resonance: \texttt{garnet} (Crystal~20, 20
qubits on a square lattice with 30 couplings) and \texttt{sirius} (Star~16,
16 qubits coupled to one central resonator). Both use a CZ two-qubit gate
with single-qubit rotations, and \texttt{sirius} adds the qubit-resonator
MOVE operation, so the circuits of Section~\ref{sec:method} compile to all
three devices unchanged. The angle schedule is fixed beforehand by the
offline calibration of Section~\ref{subsec:fixed}, so that scarce QPU time is
spent on measurement rather than on angle tuning. \rev{Every circuit is sampled
with 4096 shots at $p=1$. On \texttt{ibm\_fez} the circuits are transpiled with
the Qiskit preset pass manager at optimization level~1, which chooses the qubit
layout for each job, and executed through the Qiskit Runtime \texttt{SamplerV2}
primitive with default options, that is, without error mitigation, dynamical
decoupling or twirling. On the IQM devices the circuits are transpiled at
optimization level~1 and executed without error mitigation. The software is
Python~3.11, \texttt{qiskit}~2.5.1 and \texttt{qiskit-ibm-runtime}~0.48.0 for
IBM, and \texttt{qiskit}~2.1.2 with \texttt{iqm-client}~35.0.3 for IQM.}

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
The evaluation has two parts. The first repeats the protocol of the annealing
line on a heavy-hex processor, which places the gate rows beside the published
classical and annealing measurements on the same instances. The second changes
the coupling map and holds everything else fixed, which puts the compilation
argument of Section~\ref{subsec:depth} to a test on hardware rather than in
the compiler alone.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
We follow the protocol of the windowed annealing
study~\cite{wojewodzki2026windowed} so that the two sets of results are
directly comparable: Taillard PFSP instances with $n=20$ jobs, ten instances
per configuration, and the same lower bounds. Within that protocol the quota
reached one machine count, so every measurement we report is on the ten $m=5$
instances; the $m=10$ and $m=20$ configurations belong to the full protocol
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}. Each
neighborhood drives an iterated-local-search and a simulated-annealing loop; at
every move the swap-selection QUBO of Section~\ref{sec:method} is assembled and
handed to the fixed-angle QAOA circuit in place of the annealer. Solution
quality is reported as the percentage relative deviation
$\mathrm{PRD}=(C_{\max}-\mathrm{LB})/\mathrm{LB}\times100$ from the Taillard
lower bound $\mathrm{LB}$, averaged over instances under a fixed wall-clock
budget. \rev{Every run starts from the identity permutation, and the reported
PRD belongs to the best makespan the run has seen. Each move is read out as
described in Section~\ref{subsec:move}.}

\subsection{Execution on the heavy-hex processor}
\label{subsec:hwresults}
We ran the four gate neighborhoods on \texttt{ibm\_fez} over the ten Taillard
$20\times5$ instances under both metaheuristics, eighty runs in total, consuming
about \rev{460~s} of QPU time. All windows of a move are submitted as one job,
so a move costs a single submission irrespective of how many windows the
decomposition produces.

\rev{The runs were started with the longest budget of the protocol, 10~s, and
every one of the eighty runs completed exactly one move and overshot that
budget: measured run times ranged from 10 to 288~s. The overshoot is dominated
by queueing and job overhead rather than by computation, since the billed QPU
time per move is about 3~s for the linear neighborhoods and 7~s for the
windowed ones. A run performs at least one move, so no shorter budget could
admit a second move either. Table~\ref{tab:rpd_hw} therefore reports a single
value per neighborhood, the PRD after one move, beside the PRD of the identity
start and the median wall-clock time of the move.}

Adjacent returns 25.04 under both metaheuristics because both loops start from
the identity permutation, and the choice of metaheuristic cannot matter before a
second move. \rev{For the other three neighborhoods the ILS and SA means differ
by less than half a percentage point, while single instances differ by up to
5.8 points. With one move from the same start the two loops perform the same
computation, so these differences come from sampling on the device. The
standard deviation in Table~\ref{tab:rpd_hw} measures how much the instances
differ, not measurement noise; compared instance by instance, Dynasearch beats
Adjacent on eight of the ten instances, Fibonacci and Motzkin on seven, while
the three multi-swap neighborhoods cannot be separated from one another at this
sample size.}

The same circuits run on two further processors, with different coupling maps,
in Section~\ref{subsec:arch}.

\subsection{Comparison with the annealing back-ends}
\label{subsec:cmp}
Table~\ref{tab:cmp} places the gate rows beside the classical and annealing
measurements of the windowed study on the same instances\rev{; the classical and
annealing columns are taken at the 10~s budget, and the gate columns after one
move, which exceeded it}.

On Dynasearch the gate processor returns the lowest PRD of the three quantum
back-ends, 22.12 against 25.85 for the annealer and 26.24 for its windowed
variant, and it does so under both metaheuristics. Motzkin places it between
them: better than the windowed annealer, which is the variant it should be
compared with since both decompose the neighborhood into windows, and worse than
the full annealing formulation. On the two linear neighborhoods the gate rows
are the weakest of the three, by about five points.

Part of that gap is access latency rather than solution quality. An annealer
call costs on the order of \rev{1~s}, so from \rev{2~s} onward the annealing
runs convert the remaining budget into further moves, whereas a gate move costs
about \rev{30~s} and no budget in the protocol admits a second one. At the
budgets up to \rev{1~s}, where both back-ends answer after a single call, the
annealing and gate rows for the linear neighborhoods sit within a few tenths of
a point of each other \rev{in the annealing study}; the comparison in
Table~\ref{tab:cmp} is taken at the budget least favorable to the gate device.

All quantum back-ends remain far from the classical baselines at this instance
size, as they did in the annealing study.

\subsection{The same neighborhoods on three coupling maps}
\label{subsec:arch}
The depth argument of Section~\ref{subsec:depth} concerns compilation, so it
can be tested by changing the coupling map and holding everything else fixed.
We repeated the protocol under iterated local search on \texttt{garnet} and
on \texttt{sirius} with the same QUBOs, angles, shots and identity start.
For Adjacent and Fibonacci, whose moves compile to a single circuit each, the
quota covered the ten-instance protocol. The result is an equality rather than
an agreement. On all nine instances that fit the sixteen qubits of
\texttt{sirius}, Adjacent returns the same makespan there as on
\texttt{ibm\_fez}, instance by instance, and both columns read $25.79 \pm 10.26$;
the gap to Table~\ref{tab:rpd_hw} is the omitted instance, not the device.
Fibonacci agrees on six of those nine and differs by at most 0.85 percentage
points on the rest, at $24.39 \pm 8.99$ against $24.43 \pm 9.15$. A
tridiagonal or near-tridiagonal conflict graph compiles to a circuit short
enough that a heavy-hex lattice of 156 qubits and a 16-qubit resonator device
return the same answer, and the Fibonacci row of Table~\ref{tab:depth} says
why: both compile it to fourteen CZ gates.

The dense neighborhoods behave differently, and Table~\ref{tab:arch} reports
them on the first instance, which is as far as the quota reached. The runs
start from the identity permutation, whose makespan there is 1448 against a
lower bound of 1232, a PRD of 17.53, and the Heron~r2 column is recomputed
from that one instance rather than taken from the ten-instance mean. Motzkin
improves monotonically as the coupling map changes, by 3.65 points in total,
while Dynasearch does not follow that order and is worst on the square
lattice. One instance cannot separate a device effect from sampling noise, and
we report the row as measured.

Taken together the two groups describe a threshold rather than a gradient.
Adjacent (195, 153 and 90 CZ gates on the three maps) returns the same makespan
everywhere, while Motzkin (1455, 1080 and 576) drops from 16.96 to 13.31: below
some compiled depth the coupling map is invisible to the result, and above it
the map decides.

The sixteen qubits of \texttt{sirius} are also a binding constraint even at
$n=20$, because the width of the swap-selection QUBO varies between 5 and 18
variables across the ten instances and one of them does not fit.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
We have carried the neighborhood-as-QUBO idea from the quantum annealer to the
gate model\rev{, with one shared QUBO per neighborhood and angles fixed offline}. The analysis identifies compiled circuit depth as the
gate-model counterpart of embedding capacity: a conflict graph that exceeds an
annealer's connectivity also routes into a deep, noisy circuit, so the two
paradigms order the neighborhoods for feasibility in the same way for
otherwise unrelated reasons. Compiling the same four QUBOs onto a heavy-hex
lattice, two square lattices and a resonator-coupled device leaves that
ordering unchanged while moving the absolute depths by a factor of two, which
makes the argument a statement about compilation rather than about one
vendor's topology, and heavy-hex is the least favourable of the four. On
hardware the consequence is a threshold rather than a trend: the neighborhoods
that compile to short circuits return the same makespan on a 156-qubit
heavy-hex processor and on a 16-qubit resonator device, instance by instance.

What we could measure is bounded by allocation rather than by method. Every
run completed a single move irrespective of its budget, because a move costs
about \rev{30~s} of wall-clock time against \rev{3--7~s} of computation,
and the same move takes \rev{12~s} on a less heavily shared processor, so
that figure measures contention; the time dimension of the protocol therefore
collapses. The full protocol of the annealing line, ten instances at five
seeds across six budgets for both metaheuristics, costs about 550 credits of
device time per processor at the rates we measured, against the thirty a
monthly allocation provides, and we intend to run it once a larger allocation
is in place, on the same instances, seeds and budgets and on each of the
coupling maps. Scaling in $n$ carries a cost of its own, since billing tracks
the number of circuits rather than their depth: one move of a dense
neighborhood needs six circuits at $n=20$ and 166 at $n=500$, so the price
grows linearly with instance size where one annealer call covers the whole
QUBO up to its embedding limit. One question we leave open is methodological,
because a strict wall-clock criterion charges the queue wait to the algorithm
although that wait varies by an order of magnitude between identical
submissions.
