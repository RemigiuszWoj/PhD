\begin{abstract}
The permutation flow shop problem (PFSP) is a canonical NP-hard
scheduling problem whose local-search solvers spend most of their
effort evaluating neighborhood moves. Recent work has offloaded this
evaluation to quantum annealers by casting neighborhoods as QUBO
problems, yet the gate-model paradigm -- despite its flexible
connectivity -- remains largely unexplored for scheduling. This paper
introduces a gate-model framework that evaluates PFSP neighborhoods with
the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) embedded in
classical Iterated Local Search and Simulated Annealing metaheuristics,
giving a hybrid quantum--classical scheme for quantum-enhanced
scheduling. We reuse the swap-selection QUBO encoding from the annealing
line, so a single problem matrix drives either an annealer or a QAOA
circuit, and we adopt a fixed-angle QAOA whose variational angles are
pre-optimized offline; on hardware each move then costs a single circuit
execution, keeping the method viable under NISQ constraints. We
formulate all four neighborhood structures (Adjacent, Fibonacci,
Dynasearch, Motzkin) and analyze how their conflict-graph density
governs circuit depth once the QUBO is compiled onto heavy-hex
superconducting hardware. We show that depth assumes the role that
embedding capacity plays on annealers, producing the same feasibility
ordering for a different, compilation-grounded reason, and we discuss
windowed decomposition as a means of taming dense neighborhoods. The
paper sets out the evaluation methodology and the open challenges on the
path to on-hardware execution.
\keywords{combinatorial optimization, QUBO, metaheuristics, simulated
  annealing, circuit compilation on heavy-hex hardware, NISQ}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
The permutation flow shop problem (PFSP) is one of the most extensively
studied NP-hard problems in production scheduling. Given $n$ jobs that
must be processed in the same order on $m$ machines, the task is to find
a single job permutation that minimizes the makespan $C_{\max}$, the
completion time of the last job on the last machine. Because the number
of permutations grows factorially, exact methods are limited to small
instances, and practical solvers rely on local-search metaheuristics
such as iterated local search, simulated annealing, and tabu search.
These methods spend the overwhelming majority of their running time in
one inner operation: evaluating the moves of a neighborhood to decide
where to step next. The cost of this evaluation, repeated millions of
times over a search, is the dominant computational bottleneck and the
natural target for acceleration.

A recent line of work offloads that evaluation to quantum hardware by
casting each neighborhood as a quadratic unconstrained binary
optimization (QUBO) problem and solving it on a quantum
annealer~\cite{wojewodzki2026bpasts,wojewodzki2026windowed}. The
annealer selects, in a single call, the subset of moves that jointly
improve the makespan without conflicting, and the classical
metaheuristic proceeds from the returned permutation. The gate-model
paradigm, however, has remained almost untouched for scheduling, even
though it offers programmable circuits, routing to arbitrary qubit
pairs, and steadily growing access through cloud platforms such as IBM
Quantum. Whether the same neighborhood-as-QUBO idea can be carried from
the annealer to a gate-model device, and what changes when it is, is the
question this paper addresses.

This paper introduces a gate-model framework that evaluates PFSP
neighborhoods with the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm
(QAOA)~\cite{farhi2014qaoa} embedded in classical iterated local search
and simulated annealing. The framework reuses the swap-selection QUBO
encoding of the annealing line, so that a single problem matrix drives
either an annealer or a QAOA circuit and the two paradigms are compared
on identical instances. To keep the method viable on noisy
intermediate-scale hardware, we adopt a fixed-angle QAOA whose
variational angles are pre-optimized offline and then frozen, reducing
the online cost to one circuit execution per move, the same budget the
annealer spends. We formulate all four neighborhood structures used by
the annealing line and analyze how the density of their conflict graphs
governs circuit depth once the QUBO is compiled onto heavy-hex
superconducting hardware. Our central observation is that this depth
plays the role that embedding capacity plays on the annealer: it
produces the same feasibility ordering of the neighborhoods for a
different, compilation-grounded reason, and windowed decomposition tames
the dense neighborhoods just as it does on the annealer.

This work addresses the evaluation methodology and its analysis rather
than a large-scale hardware benchmark, and two constraints justify that
focus. Current gate-model devices are noisy and shallow, so a circuit
that is slightly too deep returns almost random samples. It therefore
matters to know in advance which neighborhoods compile to shallow
circuits. Cloud access is also metered in minutes per month, which rules
out the many exploratory executions that a variational QAOA would need
and makes the offline fixed-angle design necessary. The framework already
produces results that transfer beyond one device. A single swap-selection
QUBO drives both an annealer and a QAOA circuit, so the neighborhoods and
their penalties carry over unchanged and only the solver back-end
changes. The depth argument then yields a hardware-agnostic rule for
selecting neighborhoods. A neighborhood that fits an annealer's
connectivity also compiles to a shallow gate-model circuit. Simulation enters
only once, offline, to fix the QAOA angles; every result we report is
measured on the quantum processor.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:related}
The permutation flow shop problem has served as a testbed for scheduling
algorithms since Taillard released the benchmark instances that remain in
standard use today~\cite{taillard1993}. The problem is strongly NP-hard
once three or more machines are involved, as established by Garey,
Johnson, and Sethi~\cite{garey1976}, which is why practical solvers rely
on heuristics. Among constructive heuristics, the method of Nawaz,
Enscore, and Ham is still regarded as the strongest of its kind and is
the usual starting point for local search~\cite{neh1983}. Ruiz and Maroto
compared a large collection of heuristics and metaheuristics for the
makespan objective and confirmed that iterated local search and simulated
annealing are among the most effective approaches~\cite{ruizmaroto2005}.
These two metaheuristics, which host the quantum subroutine in our
framework, trace back to this literature: simulated annealing was
introduced as a general optimization method by Kirkpatrick, Gelatt, and
Vecchi~\cite{kirkpatrick1983}, and iterated local search was later
formalized by Louren\c{c}o, Martin, and St\"{u}tzle~\cite{lourenco2003}.
The power of such methods depends on how large a neighborhood they can
search at each step. Ahuja, Ergun, Orlin, and Punnen surveyed very
large-scale neighborhood search, in which a neighborhood of exponential
size is explored in polynomial time~\cite{ahuja2002}. Congram, Potts, and
van de Velde introduced dynasearch, which uses dynamic programming to
apply several independent interchange moves in a single step and gives
its name to the Dynasearch neighborhood studied here~\cite{congram2002}.

A more recent direction replaces the classical evaluation of these
neighborhoods with a quantum subroutine, encoding each neighborhood as a
QUBO whose solution selects a set of compatible improving swaps. One
study formulated classical and quantum versions of several flow shop
neighborhoods and compared them inside iterated local search and
simulated annealing~\cite{wojewodzki2026bpasts}. A companion paper
introduced the Motzkin neighborhood, whose non-crossing structure
produces a sparser conflict graph than the overlap-based
alternatives~\cite{wojewodzki2026soco}. A third made the approach
scalable through a windowed QUBO decomposition that keeps every
subproblem within the connectivity of the
annealer~\cite{wojewodzki2026windowed}. The present work adopts these
QUBO encodings without change and studies their behavior on gate-model
hardware.

The mapping from combinatorial problems to quadratic binary form rests on
the Ising model, and Lucas catalogued Ising formulations for a broad
range of NP problems while bounding the number of spins each
requires~\cite{lucas2014ising}. Quantum annealing is the physical process
these formulations target; Kadowaki and Nishimori introduced it as a
transverse-field method for driving an Ising system toward its ground
state~\cite{kadowaki1998}. On real hardware, Venturelli, Marchand, and
Rojo implemented job-shop scheduling on a D-Wave annealer and reported
both its promise and the embedding limits that constrain problem
size~\cite{venturelli2015}.

The gate-model counterpart of quantum annealing for optimization is the
quantum approximate optimization algorithm of Farhi, Goldstone, and
Gutmann, which alternates cost and mixer layers whose angles are tuned to
minimize the objective~\cite{farhi2014qaoa}. Zhou, Wang, Choi, Pichler,
and Lukin analyzed its optimization landscape on near-term devices and
found that good angles follow a regular pattern rather than scattering
across the parameter space~\cite{zhou2020}. Wurtz and Lykov turned this
regularity into fixed-angle conjectures that supply near-optimal
parameters without instance-specific tuning~\cite{wurtz2021fixed}, and
Galda, Liu, Lykov, Alexeev, and Safro showed that optimal angles transfer
between graphs that share local structure~\cite{galda2021}. Together
these results are what make the offline, fixed-angle design of our
framework possible. The closest gate-model work on scheduling is that of
Amaro, Rosenkranz, Fitzpatrick, Hirano, and Fiorentini, who ran several
variational quantum heuristics, QAOA among them, on a small job-shop
instance and found convergence on present hardware difficult~\cite{amaro2022}.
Our neighborhood-level encoding keeps each circuit small, which is
precisely what makes a fixed-angle approach practical where an
end-to-end formulation struggles.

The hardware itself determines which circuits can run at all. Preskill
characterized the noisy intermediate-scale quantum regime, in which noise
sharply limits the depth of a reliable circuit~\cite{preskill2018}. IBM's
superconducting processors are built on the heavy-hexagonal lattice of
Chamberland, Zhu, Yoder, Hertzberg, and Cross, which reduces frequency
collisions at the cost of sparse qubit connectivity~\cite{chamberland2020}.
Compiling a QUBO with many couplings onto such a lattice therefore
requires routing, and Li, Ding, and Xie gave an efficient qubit-mapping
and routing method whose inserted SWAP gates are exactly what deepen the
circuits analyzed here~\cite{li2019}. Circuit depth on this hardware thus
assumes the role that embedding capacity plays on the annealer, which is
the link this paper develops.

\section{Research Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

Our framework leaves the classical metaheuristic untouched and replaces
only the neighborhood-evaluation oracle with a gate-model quantum
subroutine. Every move evaluation is cast as a QUBO instance, mapped to
an Ising Hamiltonian, and solved approximately by a fixed-angle QAOA
circuit~\cite{farhi2014qaoa}. This mirrors the annealing line of
work~\cite{wojewodzki2026bpasts,wojewodzki2026windowed}: a single
swap-selection QUBO drives either a quantum annealer or a gate-model
QAOA circuit, so only the solver backend changes and the two paradigms
are compared on identical instances. The quantum subroutine is embedded,
unchanged, in the same Iterated Local Search and Simulated Annealing
hosts used by the annealing line.

\subsection{Swap-selection QUBO encoding}
\label{subsec:qubo}
We reuse the swap-selection encoding of the annealing
line~\cite{wojewodzki2026bpasts}. A binary variable $x_k=1$ indicates
that swap candidate $k$ is applied to the current permutation. Selecting
a subset of compatible swaps is written as the minimization of a
quadratic form
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{K}}\
  \mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T} Q\,\mathbf{x},
  \label{eq:qubo}
\end{equation}
where the diagonal entry $Q_{kk}=\delta_k$ is the makespan change induced
by swap $k$, evaluated in $O(1)$ time through the Head--Tail
decomposition, and the off-diagonal entry $Q_{kl}=\lambda$ penalizes
every conflicting pair of swaps. The penalty
\begin{equation}
  \lambda \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{K}\lvert\delta_k\rvert \;+\; 1
  \label{eq:penalty}
\end{equation}
dominates the objective, so any solution that selects two conflicting
swaps is more expensive than any feasible one. The pattern of penalized
pairs is the fingerprint of the neighborhood
(Section~\ref{subsec:neigh}). Crucially, $Q$ in~\eqref{eq:qubo} is the
same matrix submitted to the quantum annealer in the companion
work~\cite{wojewodzki2026windowed}; only the solver that follows differs.

\subsection{From the QUBO to a QAOA circuit}
\label{subsec:circuit}
The change of variables $Z_k=2x_k-1$, i.e.\ $x_k=(1+Z_k)/2$, turns the
QUBO~\eqref{eq:qubo} into an Ising cost
Hamiltonian~\cite{lucas2014ising}
\begin{equation}
  H_C \;=\; \sum_{i} h_i Z_i \;+\; \sum_{i<j} J_{ij} Z_i Z_j
  \;+\; \mathrm{const},
  \label{eq:ising}
\end{equation}
whose linear fields $h_i$ and couplings $J_{ij}$ are obtained directly
from the entries of $Q$. A depth-$p$ QAOA prepares the parameterized
state
\begin{equation}
  \lvert\gamma,\beta\rangle \;=\;
  \Bigl(\textstyle\prod_{l=1}^{p}
        e^{-i\beta_l H_M}\,e^{-i\gamma_l H_C}\Bigr)
  \lvert +\rangle^{\otimes K},
  \qquad H_M=\sum_i X_i,
  \label{eq:qaoa}
\end{equation}
with the transverse-field mixer $H_M$. For $p=1$ each layer compiles to
elementary gates. An initial layer of Hadamard gates places the register
in an equal superposition over all move selections. The cost evolution
$e^{-i\gamma H_C}$ is realized as a single-qubit rotation
$R_Z(2\gamma h_i)$ on every variable together with a two-qubit rotation
$R_{ZZ}(2\gamma J_{ij})$ on every coupled pair, and the mixer
$e^{-i\beta H_M}$ as a rotation $R_X(2\beta)$ on every qubit. Sampling
the circuit and taking the most frequent bitstring yields the selected
swap set, that is, the chosen neighborhood move. Figure~\ref{fig:qaoa_fib}
shows the resulting $p=1$ circuit for a small Fibonacci instance. The set
of $R_{ZZ}$ gates is exactly the edge set of the conflict graph of $Q$,
so the neighborhood structure alone determines the two-qubit layer of the
circuit.

\subsection{Fixed-angle QAOA with offline angle optimization}
\label{subsec:fixed}
Variational QAOA re-optimizes the angles $(\gamma,\beta)$ for every
instance, spending tens to hundreds of circuit executions before a single
move is chosen. Under NISQ noise and a tight quantum-runtime budget---the
IBM Open plan grants roughly ten minutes of QPU time per twenty-eight-day
window---this is not viable inside a metaheuristic that evaluates
thousands of moves. We therefore fix the angles per neighborhood type and
obtain them offline, once. For each of the four neighborhoods a single
angle pair $(\gamma^\star,\beta^\star)$ is optimized on a classical
statevector simulation by minimizing the expected cost
\begin{equation}
  (\gamma^\star,\beta^\star)
  \;=\; \arg\min_{\gamma,\beta}\
  \langle\gamma,\beta\lvert H_C\rvert\gamma,\beta\rangle,
  \label{eq:angleopt}
\end{equation}
averaged over a small training set of representative QUBO instances of
that neighborhood. The angles are then frozen and reused for every move
of that neighborhood at run time, so the offline cost is amortized over
the whole search and the online cost per move stays at a single circuit
execution. This strategy relies on angle transferability: for a fixed
neighborhood the optimal $p=1$ angles are near-constant across instances,
a property this design relies on. As a
training-free baseline we also report the instance-independent $p=1$
angles given by the fixed-angle conjectures of Wurtz and
Lykov~\cite{wurtz2021fixed}. On hardware each move therefore costs one
circuit execution, the direct gate-model analog of the annealer's single
call per move, which is the basis for a fair wall-clock comparison. We
work throughout at $p=1$, the depth that current NISQ hardware supports
reliably. Whether greater depths pay off on hardware is a question for future
work: it requires many more circuit executions than a queued, quota-limited
account allows, and therefore better access to the device than we had here.

\subsection{Neighborhoods and their conflict graphs}
\label{subsec:neigh}
All four neighborhoods reuse their annealing
QUBOs~\cite{wojewodzki2026bpasts,wojewodzki2026soco} and differ only in
the coupling pattern of the $R_{ZZ}$ layer in~\eqref{eq:qaoa}, that is,
in the topology of the conflict graph. The Adjacent neighborhood selects
one of the $n-1$ adjacent transpositions under a one-hot constraint, so
every pair of variables conflicts and its conflict graph is complete. The
Fibonacci neighborhood penalizes only consecutive overlapping swaps and
so yields a tridiagonal chain, the sparsest of the four. The Dynasearch
and Motzkin neighborhoods both act on endpoint-swap intervals but apply
different conflict rules: Dynasearch penalizes every pair of overlapping
intervals, nested ones included, whereas Motzkin admits nested intervals
and penalizes only crossings and shared endpoints. Motzkin's conflict
graph is therefore a subgraph of Dynasearch's and is strictly sparser,
which is precisely the property that motivates the Motzkin
neighborhood~\cite{wojewodzki2026soco}. The Adjacent and Dynasearch
graphs are dense and compile to the deepest circuits, while the Fibonacci
and Motzkin graphs are sparse and stay shallow.
Figure~\ref{fig:neigh_circuits} shows example $p=1$ circuits for the
Adjacent, Dynasearch, and Motzkin neighborhoods; the Fibonacci circuit is
the one in Fig.~\ref{fig:qaoa_fib}. Each $R_{ZZ}$ gate corresponds to one
edge of the conflict graph, so the two-qubit gate count is read directly
off the graph.

\subsection{Compilation on heavy-hex hardware}
\label{subsec:depth}
On IBM Heron the physical qubit graph is heavy-hex, so each $R_{ZZ}$ gate
requires its two qubits to be physically adjacent. Non-adjacent pairs are
routed by inserting SWAP networks, which deepen the circuit and inject
additional noise. Circuit depth after transpilation therefore plays the
role that embedding capacity plays on the annealer: a dense conflict
graph such as Adjacent's or Dynasearch's compiles to a deep, noisy
circuit, whereas a sparse one such as Fibonacci's or Motzkin's stays
shallow. The resulting feasibility
ordering of the four neighborhoods matches the one observed on the
annealer, but for a different, compilation-grounded reason. For the dense
neighborhoods we reuse the windowed decomposition of the annealing
line~\cite{wojewodzki2026windowed}: solving the QUBO on small overlapping
windows keeps each per-window circuit shallow enough to run within the
coherence budget of the hardware.

\subsection{Hardware and software}
\label{subsec:hw}
The circuits target the IBM Heron~r2 processor \texttt{ibm\_fez} (156
qubits, heavy-hex coupling map, native gate set CZ, RZ, SX, X), accessed
through Qiskit Runtime (\texttt{qiskit}~2.5.1 with
\texttt{qiskit-ibm-runtime}). The angle schedule is fixed
beforehand by the offline calibration of Section~\ref{subsec:fixed}, so that
scarce QPU time is spent on measurement rather than on angle tuning; all
reported results are measured on \texttt{ibm\_fez}.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
We evaluate on Taillard PFSP instances with $n=20$ jobs and $m\in\{5,10,20\}$
machines, ten instances per size, under the protocol of the windowed
annealing study~\cite{wojewodzki2026windowed} so that the two sets of results
are directly comparable. Each
neighborhood drives an iterated-local-search and a simulated-annealing loop; at
every move the swap-selection QUBO of Section~\ref{sec:method} is assembled and
handed to the fixed-angle QAOA circuit in place of the annealer. Solution
quality is reported as the relative percentage deviation
$\mathrm{RPD}=(C_{\max}-\mathrm{LB})/\mathrm{LB}\times100$ from the Taillard
lower bound $\mathrm{LB}$, averaged over instances and seeds under a fixed
wall-clock budget.

A move reads out the circuit by sampling it and returning the sampled bitstring
of lowest \emph{original}-QUBO energy. This energy-based readout is essential:
the single most probable bitstring of a low-depth QAOA state is typically the
all-zeros (do-nothing) assignment or a high-energy, many-bit state, neither of
which is the QUBO optimum the distribution concentrates on.

\subsection{Execution on quantum hardware}
\label{subsec:hwresults}
We ran the four gate neighborhoods on \texttt{ibm\_fez} over the ten Taillard
$20\times5$ instances under both metaheuristics, eighty runs in total, consuming
about 460 seconds of QPU time. All windows of a move are submitted as one job,
so a move costs a single submission irrespective of how many windows the
decomposition produces.

Table~\ref{tab:rpd_hw} reports the outcome at the longest budget of the
protocol, ten seconds. Every one of the eighty runs completed exactly one move,
and every run overshot its budget: measured run times ranged from 10 to 288
seconds against the ten-second limit. The overshoot is dominated by queueing and
job overhead rather than by computation, since the billed QPU time per move is
about three seconds for the linear neighborhoods and seven for the windowed
ones. Because a single move is the least any run can perform, and one move
already exceeds the longest budget, the shorter budgets of the protocol cannot
admit more than one move either; we report the measured column and leave the
remaining ones for later measurement.

Two features of the table follow from this single-move regime. Adjacent returns
the same value under both metaheuristics, 25.04, because both loops start from
the identity permutation and the choice of metaheuristic cannot matter before a
second move. The differences between ILS and SA for the other three
neighborhoods amount to hundredths of a percentage point and reflect sampling
noise on the device rather than algorithmic behaviour.

Two limitations bound what we could measure. The account used here provides ten
minutes of QPU time per month, which admits roughly one budget column of the
protocol at a single seed; extending the measurement to the remaining columns,
to further seeds and to greater depths is a matter of access rather than of
method. The second limitation concerns speed and is partly ours to solve: a move
costs about thirty seconds of wall-clock time against three to seven seconds of
computation, and how much of that gap can be closed---by batching more
aggressively, by shallower circuits, or by fewer shots per circuit---is an open
engineering question.

This also raises a question of measurement that we do not regard as settled.
Charging the queue wait to the running time of the algorithm is what a strict
wall-clock criterion demands, yet that wait reflects contention among users of a
shared device rather than any property of the method, and it can differ by an
order of magnitude between two submissions of the same circuit. Whether such an
accounting is fair, and what the right one is when quantum and classical
resources are reached by such different paths, deserves to be settled before
wall-clock figures from queued devices are read as statements about algorithms.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
We have carried the neighborhood-as-QUBO idea from the quantum annealer to the
gate model. A single swap-selection matrix now drives either device, so the four
neighborhood structures, their penalties and their map-back to a permutation are
shared, and only the solver behind them changes. Freezing the QAOA angles offline
reduces the online cost to one circuit execution per move, the same budget an
annealer call consumes, which is what makes the scheme usable inside a
metaheuristic that evaluates thousands of moves.

The analysis identifies compiled circuit depth as the gate-model counterpart of
embedding capacity. A conflict graph that exceeds an annealer's connectivity
also routes into a deep, noisy circuit on heavy-hex hardware, so the two
paradigms order the neighborhoods for feasibility in the same way, for reasons
that are otherwise unrelated. Motzkin's nested moves keep its conflict graph
sparser than Dynasearch's and it compiles to the shallower circuit of the two,
while windowed decomposition brings the dense neighborhoods within reach on
either device.

Executing the framework on an IBM Heron processor confirms that the pipeline
works end to end and returns a measurement over ten Taillard instances, but it
also exposes what currently governs such experiments. Every run completed a
single move irrespective of the budget it was given, because a move costs about
thirty seconds of wall-clock time against three to seven seconds of computation.
The time dimension of the protocol therefore collapses: at this access latency
the budget does not change what the algorithm does.

Three questions follow, and none of them is about the algorithm. The first is
how far the per-move cost can be driven down by batching, shallower circuits or
fewer shots. The second is how much of the protocol can be measured at all under
a metered monthly quota, which presently admits about one budget column at one
seed. The third is methodological and we leave it open: a strict wall-clock
criterion charges the queue wait to the algorithm, yet that wait measures
contention on a shared device and varies by an order of magnitude between
identical submissions. Until quantum and classical resources are reached by
comparable paths, wall-clock figures from queued hardware should be read with
that in mind.
