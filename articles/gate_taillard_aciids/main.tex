\begin{abstract}
Evaluating a neighborhood on a gate-model quantum processor has so far been
confined to the smallest scheduling instances, because the swap-selection QUBO
needs one variable per candidate move and current devices hold a few dozen
qubits. We remove that ceiling with a windowed decomposition that applies to
all four neighborhoods used by the annealing line. For the one-hot Adjacent
encoding, solving one window per position and keeping the best proposal
returns the same argument of the minimum as the undecomposed problem. Every window of a move is submitted as one job, subject to a hundred-circuit cap that the provider reveals only on submission.
The method reaches the entire Taillard benchmark on the sixteen physical qubits
of the IQM \texttt{sirius} processor, and we
report 225 runs covering all twelve configurations from twenty to five hundred jobs, to our knowledge the first measurement of gate-model neighborhoods
beyond twenty. Deviation
from the lower bound follows the ratio of machines to jobs more closely than
either alone, and a paired comparison over 48 instances places the dense
neighborhoods ahead of the linear ones in 38 of them. Against a classical loop
on the same instances the margin falls from 8.7 to 1.1 as instances grow,
because the classical search runs out of budget while the gate pipeline stays
flat. Compared with the D-Wave annealer, the gate pipeline is the only quantum
back-end that reaches every neighborhood at every
size of the earlier scaling study, and it wins every dense cell the two share.
\keywords{Permutation flow shop \and QAOA \and Huge neighborhoods
          \and Quantum optimization \and Task scheduling}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The permutation flow shop problem remains strongly NP-hard for three or more
machines, and practical solvers evaluate neighborhoods millions of times inside
local search. Recent work replaces that evaluation with a quantum subroutine,
either on an annealer or on a gate processor. Every gate-model study we know of
stops at twenty jobs, because the swap-selection QUBO needs $K = n-1$ variables
for the linear neighborhoods and $O(n^2)$ for the dense ones, while current
devices hold sixteen to fifty-four qubits.

This paper removes that ceiling. A windowed decomposition carries all four
neighborhoods to the entire Taillard range on a physical sixteen-qubit IQM
processor, and the
measurements that follow extend gate-model neighborhoods beyond $n = 20$. The results also show where the comparison protocol inherited from the
annealing line breaks down, since from fifty jobs on one neighborhood evaluation already costs more
wall-clock time than the longest budget under which the classical baselines were
measured.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
Scheduling $n$ jobs through $m$ machines in one shared order is strongly
NP-hard once $m$ reaches three~\cite{garey1976}, and the benchmark that
settled how such methods are compared is Taillard's~\cite{taillard1993}, whose
twelve configurations and lower bounds this paper uses throughout. Ruiz and
Maroto surveyed the heuristics built on it and found local search dominating
the field~\cite{ruizmaroto2005}, which puts the weight on what a single move is
allowed to change. Congram, Potts and van de Velde widened that move into
dynasearch, where a dynamic program selects several independent interchanges at
once~\cite{congram2002}; Ahuja and colleagues placed such constructions in the
general setting of very large-scale neighborhoods, searched implicitly rather
than enumerated~\cite{ahuja2002}. The Motzkin neighborhood takes its arc
structure from the paths Aigner counts~\cite{aigner1998}, and is the densest of
the four studied here.

A move set that large is only tractable because most of it can be discarded
before it is examined. Nowicki and Smutnicki showed that a swap outside the
critical blocks cannot improve the makespan~\cite{nowicki1996}, a property
Smutnicki later placed on firmer worst-case ground~\cite{smutnicki1998}, and
Wodecki and Bo\.{z}ejko discard in addition every move whose delta is not
negative~\cite{wodecki04}. The first of them matters here for a reason it was
not designed for: it decides how many candidate variables a quantum device is
asked to hold, and that count, not $n$, is what the decomposition of
Section~\ref{sec:windowed} has to fit.

Casting that selection as a quadratic binary program follows Lucas's catalogue
of Ising formulations~\cite{lucas2014ising}, and the machine that reads such a
matrix directly is the annealer of Kadowaki and
Nishimori~\cite{kadowaki1998}. Venturelli, Marchand and Rojo mapped whole job
shop instances onto one~\cite{venturelli2015}, which runs into the qubit budget
quickly, and Bo\.{z}ejko and colleagues instead handed the annealer the
neighborhood rather than the schedule~\cite{bozejko2026new}. Our own line follows
that second route: the four swap-selection matrices and their comparison on an
annealer~\cite{wojewodzki2026bpasts}, the Motzkin neighborhood in
detail~\cite{wojewodzki2026soco}, its composite counterpart under tabu search
and simulated annealing~\cite{wojewodzki2026icaisc}, the combinatorial account
of the Motzkin arcs~\cite{wojewodzki2025exit}, and the windowed decomposition
that carried the dense neighborhoods to $n = 500$ on D-Wave
hardware~\cite{wojewodzki2026windowed}.

The gate model reads the same matrix through QAOA~\cite{farhi2014qaoa}, whose
behavior at shallow depth Zhou and colleagues analyzed in
detail~\cite{zhou2020}. Tuning its angles per instance makes the algorithm expensive inside a
metaheuristic, and two results remove that cost:
Galda and colleagues showed the optimal angles transfer between instances of
one problem family~\cite{galda2021}, and Wurtz and Lykov gave angles that need
no training at all~\cite{wurtz2021fixed}. Within our department, Bo\.{z}ejko
and colleagues already applied hybrid QAOA to the flow shop itself on an IBM
processor~\cite{bozejko2024cinti}. That leaves the hardware, whose
limits Preskill set out for this era~\cite{preskill2018} and which we met in a
form rarely reported: a cap on circuits per job and a move that outlasts the
budget it was measured under. The gate-model study closest to this one is our
own~\cite{wojewodzki2026gcmm}, which stops at twenty jobs because it has no
decomposition.

\section{Problem and QUBO Formulation}
\label{sec:method}
An instance gives $n$ jobs and $m$ machines, each job passing the machines in
the same order, and a solution is a permutation $\pi$ of the jobs whose
makespan $C_{\max}(\pi)$ is the moment the final job clears the final
machine. The four neighborhoods differ in how far a single move reaches along $\pi$.
Adjacent exchanges one pair of consecutive jobs, and Fibonacci applies a set of
pairwise disjoint consecutive exchanges. Dynasearch selects disjoint
intervals and Motzkin non-crossing ones that may nest, and both exchange the
endpoints of every selected interval. 

For every candidate move the makespan change $\delta_k$ follows in constant
time from the Head and Tail profiles of the current permutation, so a whole
candidate set is priced in one pass. The hardware pipeline applies only the block property of Nowicki and Smutnicki, which discards moves that cannot touch the critical path. As in the enhanced
annealing variants~\cite{wojewodzki2026windowed}, moves with $\delta_k \ge 0$
stay in the QUBO, where their
non-negative diagonal entries already make them unattractive to the solver.
The block property matters for what follows, because it makes the number of
surviving candidates depend on the instance rather than on $n$ alone, and that
number is what a device has to hold.

\subsection{Swap-selection QUBO}
\label{subsec:qubo}
Let the neighborhood supply $K$ candidates and let $x_k \in \{0,1\}$ record
whether candidate $k$ is applied, for $k = 1, \dots, K$. Choosing a compatible
subset is the quadratic program
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{x} \in \{0,1\}^{K}} \mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T} Q \mathbf{x},
  \qquad Q_{kk} = \delta_k, \qquad
  Q_{kl} = \lambda \ \text{ for } 1 \le k < l \le K \text{ in conflict},
  \label{eq:qubo}
\end{equation}
with $Q$ upper triangular so that each conflicting pair is charged once, and
with $\lambda = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \lvert \delta_k \rvert + 1$ large enough that no
infeasible selection can beat a feasible one. Adjacent is the one exception:
there every pair conflicts, so the constraint becomes one-hot and the penalty
moves onto the diagonal as well, with $Q_{kk} = \delta_k - P$ and
$Q_{kl} = 2P$, where $P = 2\max_k \lvert \delta_k \rvert + 1$.

Which pairs are charged is the only thing that separates the four
neighborhoods, and it also decides whether the matrix survives being cut down to
a window. Fibonacci charges a pair only when the two exchanges
touch the same position, which makes $Q$ tridiagonal, and Adjacent charges
every pair. Both patterns depend on the candidates alone, so restricting them
to a subset of positions yields exactly the matrix the full problem would have
assigned to that subset. The interval neighborhoods charge a pair when two intervals overlap, for
Dynasearch, or cross or share an endpoint, for Motzkin, a relation between
candidates, not positions,
and Section~\ref{sec:windowed} treats them separately for that reason.

\subsection{Fixed-angle QAOA}
\label{subsec:qaoa}
Substituting $x_k = (Z_k + 1)/2$ turns~\eqref{eq:qubo} into an Ising
Hamiltonian $H_C$ over $K$ spins, and QAOA prepares
$\lvert \psi \rangle = e^{-i\beta H_M} e^{-i\gamma H_C} H^{\otimes K}
\lvert 0 \rangle^{K}$ at depth $p = 1$, with the transverse field
$H_M = \sum_{k=1}^{K} X_k$ as mixer and $\gamma \in [0, \pi]$,
$\beta \in [0, \pi/2]$. The two angles are optimized once per neighborhood on
a small set of representative matrices and then frozen, which keeps the online
cost of a move at one submission instead of the hundreds of circuits a
variational loop would spend before committing to a single move.

A move samples the circuit and keeps whichever assignment scores lowest
against the original $Q$, rather than whichever one came up most often. The
distinction decides whether the method works at all on a noisy device: at
$p = 1$ the modal outcome is usually the all-zeros assignment, which applies no
swap, or a high-energy string with many bits set. Energy-based readout also
makes the windowed solver of the next section well defined, since each window
is sampled and reduced independently and the winner is the assignment its own matrix scores lowest.

\subsection{Window geometry}
A window of $w$ consecutive positions yields $w-1$ variables for the
tridiagonal and one-hot matrices but $w(w-1)/2$ for the pairwise one, so one
window size cannot serve both classes. On sixteen qubits this means $w = 17$ for
the linear neighborhoods against $w = 6$ for the dense ones, and the window of
19 used by the annealing study fits no gate device, since it would need 171
variables.

Every window of one move is submitted as a single job, so the queue and the job
overhead are paid once per move rather than once per window. The exception is
the dense decomposition at $n = 500$, whose 166 windows exceed the cap of a
hundred circuits per job and go out in two jobs; a framework that batches
windows without checking the count has its job declined. A declined job returns an empty list of histograms without raising, and each
neighborhood then falls back to a classical greedy swap, so the run completes
and is stored as a hardware measurement while being entirely classical. We
detected this on the Motzkin rows of \texttt{tai50\_10}, whose moves took 2.5
seconds against the 28 seconds of the valid rows, and the guard now compares the number of returned
histograms against the number of submitted circuits.

\subsection{Tridiagonal, pairwise and one-hot windows}
Fibonacci's QUBO couples only physically adjacent swaps, so the matrix of a
position window is exactly the global matrix restricted to that window and the
decomposition leaves the formulation unchanged. Swaps straddling a window
boundary are resolved by the same non-overlap validation the single-QUBO path
uses. Consecutive windows of either class overlap by half their width. Each window enumerates the
intervals that lie inside it and builds its own pairwise matrix; the intervals
selected in all windows are then merged, and a greedy left-to-right pass drops
every interval that conflicts with one already kept.
An interval longer than a window is never a candidate, so for the dense
neighborhoods the decomposition approximates the global problem instead of
reproducing it. If no interval survives
the merge, the move falls back to the best single improving interchange,
computed classically.

The global one-hot QUBO selects the candidate of lowest delta, so solving one
one-hot QUBO per window and keeping the best proposal computes the same
$\arg\min$ and still applies exactly one swap. This matters because the D-Wave enhanced variants never decomposed the linear
neighborhoods; the annealer held the whole linear QUBO, which no gate device
can.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
To keep the rows comparable with the windowed annealing study, we reuse its
protocol: the Taillard instances with $n \in \{20, 50, 100, 200, 500\}$
jobs and $m \in \{5, 10, 20\}$ machines, the percentage relative deviation
(PRD) from Taillard's lower bound, and ten seconds of wall-clock time per run.
Each neighborhood drives iterated local search~\cite{lourenco2003}, which
perturbs and re-optimizes a single incumbent. All runs use the physical IQM \texttt{sirius} processor, reached through the IQM
Resonance cloud, with $p = 1$ and 4096 shots per circuit. The processor has
sixteen qubits in a star topology, an architecture for which Gnatowski and
colleagues give an exact circuit-routing algorithm~\cite{gnatowski2026routing}.

We depart from the annealing protocol in three places. We run only iterated
local search, since with one to three moves per run from the identity permutation it would
barely differ from simulated annealing. One run replaces five seeds. For the linear neighborhoods repeated runs only
resample shot noise, with a measured spread of 0.00 points for Adjacent and 0.03
for Fibonacci; the dense neighborhoods spread more, by 1.5 points on average over
five repetitions on instance 0 and by up to 4.1 on \texttt{tai20\_10}, which is
why their ordering rests on the paired comparison of
Section~\ref{subsec:paired} rather than on any single cell. Four instances replace ten, because the processor time
available to us covered four.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
All gate results in this section come from runs on the physical IQM
\texttt{sirius} processor; none of them is simulated. Table~\ref{tab:rpd}
reports every configuration of the benchmark, from twenty
jobs on five machines to five hundred on twenty. A dense
neighborhood takes ten of the twelve columns and Fibonacci the other two, while
Adjacent takes none and has the worst mean. That ordering follows from what a move is allowed to do:
Adjacent applies exactly one swap, the others apply several.

Deviation falls as the instances grow. The hardest column is
$20\times20$, where even the best neighborhood leaves 42.8\%, and the easiest
is $100\times5$ at 11.2\%; holding $m = 20$ and raising $n$ walks
monotonically down from 42.8 to 15.2. The rows follow the ratio $m/n$, which correlates with the best value of each
column at $0.89$, against $-0.45$ for $n$ alone and $0.55$ for $m$ alone
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ratio} separates the two). One reading of the direction is that
machines per job measures how much idle time a schedule carries, and one move
recovers a fixed amount of it, so the same move is worth less where there is
more to recover.

The move row accounts for the rest: the columns differ in problem size and also
in how many moves the same ten seconds bought.
One move takes between four seconds and four and a half minutes of wall-clock
time, depending on $n$ and on the neighborhood class, so the ten-second budget
admits one to three moves at $n = 20$ and one from $n = 50$ upward. From
$n = 50$ on, every shorter budget of the protocol therefore describes the same
computation, which is why we report the comparison per move.

\subsection{Which neighborhood wins, instance by instance}
\label{subsec:paired}
Absolute deviation varies far more between instances than between
neighborhoods: on \texttt{tai50\_5} the four instances span 12.7 points
while the four neighborhoods sit within 0.6 of each other, so
comparing column means would say little. The four neighborhoods run on the same
instances, however, so instance difficulty is a shared term that cancels in a
paired comparison, and we compare them pair by pair over the 48 combinations of
file and instance.

Against Adjacent, Fibonacci gains 1.37 points and wins 40 of the 48 pairs,
Motzkin gains 1.49 and wins 38, and Dynasearch gains 1.84 and wins 42; a
two-sided sign test puts all three below $p=0.001$. Grouped, the two dense
neighborhoods beat the two linear ones in 38 of 48 pairs ($p=0.00006$).
Counting outright winners, Motzkin and Dynasearch take nineteen pairs each,
Fibonacci seven and Adjacent three. Fig.~\ref{fig:paired} shows the four
distributions. The ranking is settled even though no column of
Table~\ref{tab:rpd} separates the four rows by more than the spread between its
own instances.

\subsection{Comparison with the classical baseline}
The same loop, the same instances and the same ten-second budget run entirely
on a CPU give Table~\ref{tab:cls}. Averaged over the four neighborhoods, the
classical search is ahead everywhere, which is the expected result and not the
interesting one.

The column of ratios shows the margin closing as the instances
grow, from 8.7 times on \texttt{tai50\_5} to 1.1 on \texttt{tai500\_20}. The
reason is not that the gate pipeline improves with $n$: its own figures move
from 14.5\% to 15.6\%, which is flat. The classical baseline degrades instead,
from 1.7\% to 13.9\%, because ten seconds no longer suffice for it to converge
once the instance is large. Both methods are then far from the lower bound, and
they are far from it for different reasons: one has run out of time and the
other has made a single move. We draw no advantage from this. A gate processor that needs 1.48 seconds per
circuit cannot compete with a loop that completes thousands of iterations in
the same budget.

\subsection{Comparison with earlier quantum back-ends}
Table~\ref{tab:prev} places the gate pipeline next to the earlier measurements
of the same four neighborhoods, the classical loop and the D-Wave annealer in its original and enhanced
variants~\cite{wojewodzki2026windowed}. It uses the scaling
columns of that study, $m = 10$ up to $n = 200$ and $m = 20$ at $n = 500$,
under a five-second budget. The gate values at five seconds are read from the
move log of the ten-second runs, which records the makespan after every move.
The rows of the earlier back-ends are recomputed from their raw runs on
instances 0--3, five seeds each, so that every row averages over the same
instances, and they therefore differ from the ten-instance values published
in~\cite{wojewodzki2026windowed}. As in the annealing study, a move started
within the budget is completed and counted, so the quantum rows overrun it: the enhanced annealer needed a median of 541 seconds for one dense move at
$n = 50$, and the gate processor 29. Our earlier gate-model
study~\cite{wojewodzki2026gcmm} measured the undecomposed pipeline at
$20 \times 5$ only, a configuration outside these columns.

The gate rows are the only quantum rows without a gap. The original annealer
stops at $n = 20$, and the enhanced variants carry it to $n = 200$ for Adjacent and to
$n = 500$ for Fibonacci, while its windowed dense neighborhoods stop at
$n = 50$. The
gate pipeline fills all twenty cells, the dense neighborhoods at $n = 500$
included. Where the gate pipeline and the enhanced
annealer both have a value they split the thirteen
cells seven to six, and the split follows the neighborhood class. The annealer
wins every Fibonacci cell, where it completes three iterations in five seconds
against the single move of the gate processor. The gate processor wins every
dense cell, by 2.3 to 5.3 points, where both make a single move, and takes
Adjacent from $n = 50$ on. The classical loop stays ahead of both everywhere.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Adjacent returns a makespan of 1390 on instance 0 of \texttt{tai20\_5} on
\texttt{garnet}, \texttt{emerald} and \texttt{sirius} alike, three different
coupling maps. The processors differ only in how many moves fit the budget, so a
cross-architecture comparison that does not hold the move count fixed measures
queue latency rather than fidelity. Gnatowski and Ni\.{z}y\'{n}ski survey how compilation for superconducting
processors depends on the coupling map~\cite{gnatowski2026mapping}.

Fourteen of the 225 hardware runs returned the starting makespan, every one of
them in a dense neighborhood, and six of the eight Motzkin runs on
\texttt{tai20\_20} did so. The deltas entering the QUBO are computed
independently, while the pairwise penalties do not capture how several swaps
compose, so a set of individually improving swaps can worsen the schedule. The
limitation lies in the formulation rather than the device, and it applies to the
annealing line as well.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
A windowed decomposition carries all four swap-selection neighborhoods to the
full Taillard benchmark on a physical sixteen-qubit IQM processor, and the 225
runs reported here extend gate-model neighborhood measurements past twenty jobs.
For the linear neighborhoods the decomposition leaves the formulation intact,
and a paired comparison over the same instances places Dynasearch 1.84 points
ahead of Adjacent on 42 of 48 pairs, with the dense pair ahead of the linear
pair on 38. Whether any of this is worth doing on present hardware remains open:
a single move already comes within a factor of about two of the classical loop,
so the gap to close is throughput, and a device that delivered moves at
classical rates would make the comparison worth repeating.
