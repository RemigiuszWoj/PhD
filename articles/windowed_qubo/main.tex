\begin{abstract}
We extend the quantum neighborhood framework for the Permutation Flow
Shop Scheduling Problem (PFSP) introduced in~\cite{WojewodzkiBozejko2026}
by introducing a third class of neighborhood structures,
\emph{quantum QUBO enhanced}, designed to operate on larger benchmark
instances than previously possible on real quantum annealing hardware.
The original framework comprised eight neighborhoods: four classical
(Adjacent, Fibonacci, Dynasearch, Motzkin) and four quantum QUBO
counterparts executable on a D-Wave quantum processing unit (QPU) for
instances with $n = 20$ jobs. The new enhanced class introduces four
additional neighborhoods that extend QPU applicability: (i)~Quantum
Adjacent Enhanced and Quantum Fibonacci Enhanced, which remove the
delta-filtering heuristic and support instances up to $n = 200$ and
$n = 500$ respectively, leveraging the linear ($K = n-1$) QUBO
variable count of these neighborhoods; and
(ii)~Quantum Dynasearch Enhanced and Quantum Motzkin Enhanced, which
introduce a windowed QUBO decomposition enabling execution on
instances up to $n = 50$, where the full $O(n^2)$-variable QUBO
would otherwise exceed QPU capacity.
All twelve neighborhoods are evaluated on standard Taillard benchmark
instances under Iterated Local Search and Simulated Annealing,
measured by Relative Percentage Deviation (RPD) from best-known
makespans across six time budgets (100--10\,000\,ms).
\keywords{Flow shop scheduling \and QUBO \and Quantum annealing \and
          D-Wave \and Neighborhood search \and Windowed decomposition}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The Permutation Flow Shop Scheduling Problem (PFSP) is a classical
NP-hard combinatorial problem~\cite{ref_bozejko_parallel} in which
$n$ jobs must be processed on $m$ machines in identical sequence,
with the objective of minimizing makespan $C_{\max}$.
Neighborhood-based local search remains the dominant paradigm for
obtaining high-quality solutions within practical time budgets.

In prior work~\cite{WojewodzkiBozejko2026}, we formulated four
classical PFSP neighborhood structures as Quadratic Unconstrained
Binary Optimization (QUBO) problems and executed them on D-Wave
quantum annealing hardware, providing the first empirical comparison
of classical and quantum neighborhood search under identical
wall-clock time budgets. The experiments were limited to Taillard
instances with $n = 20$ jobs due to the variable-count constraints of
the QUBO formulations and the effective embedding capacity of the
D-Wave Advantage QPU.

The present work addresses two limitations of that study:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No delta filter on QPU.} The original implementation
        filtered out non-improving swap candidates ($\delta_k \geq 0$)
        before submitting the QUBO to the QPU, a heuristic necessary
        for the classical simulator but not for real QPU hardware,
        which handles the full variable space natively.

  \item \textbf{Fixed instance size.} The $O(n^2)$ variable count of
        Dynasearch and Motzkin neighborhoods limited experiments to
        $n = 20$, while Adjacent and Fibonacci ($K = n-1$ variables)
        could theoretically scale to much larger instances within
        QPU capacity.
\end{enumerate}

We address both limitations by introducing the \emph{quantum QUBO
enhanced} class, comprising four new neighborhood formulations. The
resulting twelve-neighborhood taxonomy — four classical, four quantum
QUBO, four quantum QUBO enhanced — provides a structured framework
for studying how quantum optimization scales with instance size and
QUBO graph structure in a practically relevant combinatorial problem.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A windowed QUBO decomposition for Dynasearch and Motzkin
        neighborhoods, enabling QPU execution for $n \leq 50$.
  \item Delta-filter-free QUBO formulations for Adjacent and
        Fibonacci neighborhoods, supporting $n \leq 200$ and
        $n \leq 500$ respectively on D-Wave Advantage.
  \item The first empirical comparison of all three neighborhood
        classes across the full Taillard benchmark range
        ($n \in \{20, 50, 100, 200, 500\}$) under identical
        metaheuristic frameworks and time budgets.
  \item A quantitative analysis of the tractability boundary for
        each neighborhood class on current QPU hardware.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem Definition}

\paragraph{Permutation Flow Shop Problem.}
Given a matrix of processing times $P = [p_{k,j}]_{m \times n}$,
where $k = 1,\dots,m$ denotes a machine and $j = 1,\dots,n$ a job,
a permutation $\pi$ determines the order in which jobs are processed
identically on all machines. The completion time $C_{k,j}$ satisfies:
\begin{align}
  C_{1,j} &= C_{1,j-1} + p_{1,\pi(j)}, \quad
  C_{k,1} = C_{k-1,1} + p_{k,\pi(1)}, \\
  C_{k,j} &= \max\{C_{k,j-1},\, C_{k-1,j}\} + p_{k,\pi(j)}.
\end{align}
The objective is to find $\pi^* = \arg\min_\pi C_{m,n}$.

\paragraph{Metaheuristics.}
We employ Iterated Local Search (ILS)~\cite{lourenco2003iterated}
with a tabu list (tenure $\tau = 10$) and Simulated Annealing
(SA)~\cite{kirkpatrick1983} with parameters $T_{\text{init}} = 1000$,
$\alpha = 0.95$, reheating factor $r = 1.5$, stagnation threshold
$500$\,ms. Both algorithms are evaluated under identical time budgets.

\paragraph{Diversification — Mushroom List.}
ILS maintains an elite solution pool (\emph{mushroom list}) of capacity
$k = 10$ to guide restarts when all candidate moves are tabu and the
aspiration criterion is not met.
The pool stores the $k$ best distinct permutations encountered during
search, ranked by $C_{\max}$.
On a diversification event, a \emph{double-bridge} (4-opt) perturbation
is applied to the least-recently-used elite: the permutation is split
into four consecutive segments $A \mid B \mid C \mid D$ at three
uniformly sampled cut positions and reconnected as $A \mid C \mid B \mid D$.
This move lies outside the 2-opt neighbourhood and cannot be undone by
any single adjacent or endpoint swap, ensuring genuine escape from the
current basin of attraction.
The pool is updated after every improvement to $C_{\max}^*$; when the
pool is empty (early search), the fallback is a uniform random restart.
Round-robin selection across the pool ensures that successive
diversification events explore distinct elite neighbourhoods rather
than repeatedly perturbing the single global best.

\section{Neighborhood Taxonomy}

We organize the twelve neighborhoods into three classes.

\subsection{Classical Neighborhoods}

The four classical neighborhoods are identical to those
in~\cite{WojewodzkiBozejko2026,Motzkin_PWR}: Adjacent, Fibonacci,
Dynasearch, and Motzkin. All use the Head+Tail technique for $O(mn)$
delta evaluation and dynamic programming for optimal swap selection.
Their QUBO formulations are recalled in Section~\ref{sec:qubo}.

\subsection{Block-Property Accelerators}
\label{sec:accelerators}

All twelve neighborhoods apply the block-property accelerator of
Smutnicki~\cite{ref_smutnicki} to prune dominated swap candidates
before search. The accelerator rests on a structural decomposition
of the current permutation $\pi$ into \emph{blocks}: maximal
contiguous sub-sequences in which the last machine $m$ never idles.
A position $u \in \{1,\dots,n-1\}$ is a \emph{block boundary} if
\begin{equation}
  H[m-1][u] = H[m-2][u] + p_{m,\pi(u+1)},
\end{equation}
where $H[k][j]$ denotes the completion time of job $\pi(j)$ on
machine $k$ computed by the Head recurrence. The set of all block
boundaries $\mathcal{U} = \{u_1, \dots, u_q\} \subseteq \{1,\dots,n-1\}$
is computed in $O(mn)$ from the Head matrix, which is available
anyway for delta evaluation.

\paragraph{Adjacent and Fibonacci accelerator.}
For the adjacent-swap neighborhoods, swapping positions $(a, a+1)$
can improve $C_{\max}$ only if $a$ or $a+1$ coincides with a block
boundary, i.e., $\{a, a+1\} \cap \mathcal{U} \neq \emptyset$.
This filters up to $n-1$ swap candidates down to at most
$2(m-1)$ on average, reducing evaluation cost from $O(mn)$ to
$O(m \cdot \min(n, 2(m-1)))$ per iteration. The Fibonacci
neighborhood applies the same test, since it selects from the
same adjacent-swap candidates.

\paragraph{NPI accelerator for endpoint-swap neighborhoods.}
For neighborhoods that consider non-adjacent endpoint-swap pairs
$(i, j)$ with $j > i+1$ (Dynasearch, Motzkin), a generalized
\emph{No-boundary-in-span} (NPI) filter applies: the pair $(i, j)$
is pruned unless $\mathcal{U} \cap [i, j] \neq \emptyset$, i.e.,
at least one block boundary lies between positions $i$ and $j$.
The NPI filter reduces the $O(n^2)$ candidate set to at most
$O(n \cdot (m-1))$ pairs on average, matching the $O(mn)$ scaling
of delta computation. In the classical and quantum QUBO
implementations, NPI is applied before the QUBO is assembled,
so the number of QUBO variables is bounded by $O(n(m-1))$ rather
than $O(n^2)$, which directly reduces QPU embedding overhead.

In all three neighborhood classes, block boundaries are computed
once per move evaluation (reusing the Head matrix) and shared
across all candidate pairs for that iteration.

\subsection{Quantum QUBO Neighborhoods}

The four quantum QUBO neighborhoods correspond directly to the
classical structures reformulated as QUBO problems and solved on a
D-Wave Advantage QPU via the \texttt{EmbeddingComposite} sampler.
These are suitable for instances with $n \leq 20$ and are described
fully in~\cite{WojewodzkiBozejko2026}.

\subsection{Quantum QUBO Enhanced Neighborhoods}
\label{sec:enhanced}

The four enhanced neighborhoods extend QPU applicability to larger
instances through two mechanisms:

\paragraph{Full QUBO without delta filtering (Adjacent, Fibonacci).}
The original quantum QUBO implementation filtered candidate swaps
with $\delta_k \geq 0$ before QUBO construction to reduce the
variable count for the classical simulator. On real QPU hardware this
filter is unnecessary: the QPU naturally assigns $x_k = 0$ to
non-improving candidates. Removing it produces a complete QUBO that
better reflects the true optimization landscape. Since $K = n - 1$
for both neighborhoods, these formulations remain tractable on
D-Wave Advantage (${\sim}5{,}600$ physical qubits) up to $n = 200$
(Adjacent, dense $Q$) and $n = 500$ (Fibonacci, tridiagonal $Q$).

\paragraph{Windowed QUBO decomposition (Dynasearch, Motzkin).}
For Dynasearch and Motzkin, the full QUBO has $K = O(n^2)$ variables
with $O(n^4)$ interactions, exceeding QPU capacity for $n > 20$.
We introduce a windowed decomposition: the permutation $\pi$ is
partitioned into overlapping windows of size $w$, and a separate
QUBO is built and solved independently for each window. Results from
all windows are merged with greedy conflict resolution.

Formally, for window $[s, s+w)$, the QUBO is defined over the
$\binom{w}{2}$ candidate swap pairs $(i,j)$ with $s \leq i < j < s+w$.
Windows advance by step $\lfloor w(1-\rho)\rfloor$, where $\rho$ is
the overlap ratio (default $\rho = 0.5$). The window size $w$ is
chosen automatically to keep $K(w) = w(w-1)/2 \leq C_{\text{eff}}$,
where $C_{\text{eff}} = 180$ is a conservative estimate of the
effective D-Wave variable capacity for dense QUBOs:
\begin{equation}
  w_{\max} = \left\lfloor\frac{1 + \sqrt{1 + 8\,C_{\text{eff}}}}{2}\right\rfloor = 19.
  \label{eq:window}
\end{equation}
This enables Dynasearch and Motzkin Enhanced to execute on instances
up to $n = 50$.

\section{QUBO Formulations}
\label{sec:qubo}

All neighborhoods share the general QUBO form:
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^K}\;\mathbf{x}^\top Q\,\mathbf{x}
  \;\xrightarrow{x_i=(1-Z_i)/2}\;
  H_C = \sum_i h_i Z_i + \sum_{i<j} J_{ij} Z_i Z_j.
  \label{eq:qubo}
\end{equation}
Diagonal entries $Q_{kk} = \delta_k$ encode the makespan change from
swap $k$, computed via the Head+Tail technique. Off-diagonal entries
$Q_{kl} = P$ penalize conflicting swap pairs, with
$P = \sum_k |\delta_k| + 1$ ensuring constraint dominance.

\paragraph{Adjacent.} One-hot QUBO: $Q_{ii} = \delta_i - P$,
$Q_{ij} = 2P$ (all pairs), enforcing selection of exactly one swap.

\paragraph{Fibonacci.} Tridiagonal QUBO: $Q_{ii} = \delta_i$,
$Q_{i,i+1} = P$; only $n-2$ nonzero off-diagonal entries. The
number of feasible swap sets equals the Fibonacci number $F_{n+1}$.

\paragraph{Dynasearch.} $Q_{kl} = P$ iff segments $(i_1,j_1)$ and
$(i_2,j_2)$ overlap: $\max(i_1,i_2) \leq \min(j_1,j_2)$. Nesting
is forbidden.

\paragraph{Motzkin.} $Q_{kl} = P$ iff pairs conflict under Motzkin
rules: shared endpoint or crossing arcs. Nesting and disjoint pairs
are unconstrained ($Q_{kl} = 0$). The number of feasible swap sets
equals the Motzkin number $M_n$~\cite{Motzkin_PWR}.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Benchmarks.}
We use standard Taillard benchmark
instances~\cite{ref_taillard}: $n \in \{20, 50, 100, 200, 500\}$,
$m \in \{5, 10, 20\}$. For each $(n, m)$ configuration, 10 instances
are used. Time budgets: $t_{\max} \in \{100, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000,
10\,000\}$\,ms. Seeds are fixed for reproducibility.

\paragraph{Hardware.}
Classical neighborhoods run on an Apple M4 Pro (24\,GB RAM).
Quantum QUBO and Enhanced neighborhoods use the D-Wave Advantage QPU
via the Leap cloud platform, with \texttt{EmbeddingComposite} sampler
and $\mathtt{num\_reads} = 100$.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Solution quality is measured by Relative Percentage Deviation from
the best-known Taillard makespan:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RPD} = \frac{C_{\max} - C^*}{C^*} \times 100\%.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Instance constraints.}
Following the tractability analysis in Table~\ref{tab:feasibility}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Quantum QUBO (original): $n = 20$ only.
  \item Quantum Adjacent Enhanced: $n \leq 200$.
  \item Quantum Fibonacci Enhanced: $n \leq 500$.
  \item Quantum Dynasearch/Motzkin Enhanced: $n \leq 50$,
        window size $w = 19$ (auto), overlap $\rho = 0.5$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Comparison at $n = 20$.}
Table~\ref{tab:rpd_n20} compares all twelve neighborhoods on the
smallest common instance size. The quantum QUBO enhanced variants
are expected to perform comparably to the original quantum QUBO
variants at $n = 20$, since the only difference is the removal of
the delta filter — a change that affects the QUBO landscape but not
the variable count or embedding structure. Any observed difference
will quantify the effect of the filter on solution quality.

\paragraph{Scaling behavior.}
Table~\ref{tab:rpd_scaling} examines how the enhanced quantum
neighborhoods scale with $n$. The primary hypothesis is that Quantum
Fibonacci Enhanced, with its tridiagonal QUBO structure and linear
variable count, will maintain competitive RPD relative to classical
Fibonacci across all instance sizes, while Quantum Adjacent Enhanced
will show increasing RPD for $n > 100$ due to its dense QUBO graph
requiring more physical qubits and deeper embedding chains.

\paragraph{Windowed decomposition quality.}
The windowed approach for Dynasearch and Motzkin Enhanced introduces
suboptimality relative to the full QUBO: swaps crossing window
boundaries are not considered. The magnitude of this quality loss
will be measured by comparing Enhanced RPD against classical
Dynasearch/Motzkin at $n = 20$ (where both formulations are
feasible) and quantified as a function of window overlap ratio $\rho$.

\paragraph{Throughput analysis.}
A key factor in quantum neighborhood performance is the number of
neighborhood evaluations per second achievable within a fixed time
budget. The enhanced formulations submit more QUBO calls per
iteration (one per window) compared to the original formulations
(one per iteration). The tradeoff between larger effective
neighborhood coverage and increased QPU latency will be quantified.

\section{Conclusions}

We introduced the quantum QUBO enhanced neighborhood class, extending
quantum annealing-based PFSP neighborhood search to larger instances
through two complementary mechanisms: delta-filter removal for
linearly-scaling QUBO structures (Adjacent, Fibonacci) and windowed
QUBO decomposition for quadratically-scaling structures (Dynasearch,
Motzkin).

The resulting twelve-neighborhood taxonomy provides a systematic
framework for studying the interaction between QUBO graph structure,
instance size, and metaheuristic performance in quantum combinatorial
optimization. Preliminary analysis suggests that the tractability
boundary varies substantially across neighborhoods: Fibonacci
Enhanced remains feasible up to $n = 500$ on current D-Wave hardware,
while Motzkin and Dynasearch Enhanced are limited to $n = 50$ by the
windowed decomposition constraint.

\paragraph{Future work.} Directions include: adaptive window sizing
based on convergence feedback, integration of QAOA circuits on
gate-based hardware (Willow processor), and investigation of
alternative decomposition strategies that preserve cross-window swap
interactions.
