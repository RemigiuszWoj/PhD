\title{Scalable Quantum Neighborhood Search for the Permutation Flow Shop
       Problem via Windowed QUBO Decomposition}

\author[pwr]{Remigiusz Wojew\'{o}dzki\corref{cor1}}

  city={Wroc\l{}aw}, country={Poland}}

\begin{abstract}
Quantum annealers can evaluate an entire scheduling neighborhood in a
single call, but so far only for the smallest benchmark instances. For
the permutation flow shop problem we introduce an enhanced class of
quantum QUBO neighborhoods that lifts this limit from 20 to 500 jobs:
an unnecessary candidate filter is removed from the linearly
structured neighborhoods, and the quadratic ones --- including our
Motzkin neighborhood, whose moves follow Motzkin-path combinatorics
--- are decomposed into overlapping windows solved on the processor
piece by piece. Measurements on a real D-Wave annealer confirm the
design: quantum solution quality improves steadily with instance size,
and the gap to the classical baselines narrows several-fold, reaching
parity at the largest instances. Windowing proves nearly free where
move interactions are local, while for Motzkin --- whose strength lies
in globally nested moves --- its cost grows with the time budget. The
paper also reports the measurement methodology required to compare
quantum and classical search fairly under wall-clock budgets.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
  \item Quantum QUBO enhanced class breaks the $n=20$ D-Wave QPU barrier for PFSP search.
  \item Delta filter removal extends Adjacent/Fibonacci QUBO to $n\leq 200$ and $n\leq 500$.
  \item Windowed QUBO decomposition enables Dynasearch and Motzkin on instances up to $n=50$.
  \item Smutnicki accelerator cuts QUBO variable count by 30--60\% before QPU submission.
  \item Quantum-to-classical RPD gap narrows from 20.4\,pp at $n=20$ to 4.6\,pp at $n=500$ on QPU.
  \item Windowing is near-free for local move structures; its loss compounds for global ones.
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}

The Permutation Flow Shop Scheduling Problem (PFSP) asks for a
permutation of $n$ jobs on $m$ machines in series that minimizes
makespan $C_{\max}$. The problem is NP-hard for
$m \geq 3$~\citep{garey76}, and for large benchmark instances the best
known solutions are produced by neighborhood-based metaheuristics
rather than exact methods~\citep{bozejko2010}. The choice of
neighborhood structure --- the set of solutions reachable from the
current one by a single admissible
modification~\citep{vanlaarhoven1992} --- is therefore a primary
driver of solution quality.

The classical PFSP literature is long and well charted. Johnson's
algorithm solves the two-machine case in polynomial
time~\citep{johnson54}; on the constructive side, the NEH
heuristic~\citep{neh83} remains the standard starting point four
decades after its publication. The metaheuristic line moved through
simulated annealing~\citep{osman89} and genetic
algorithms~\citep{reeves95}, but its backbone is tabu search:
Taillard's implementation~\citep{taillard90}, the block-based fast
tabu search of Nowicki and Smutnicki~\citep{nowicki96}, and its
accelerated successor by Grabowski and Wodecki~\citep{grabowski04}
successively cut both the runtime and the distance to optimum on the
benchmark set of~\citep{ref_taillard}. Later work consolidated the
field: the review of Ruiz and Maroto~\citep{ruizmaroto05} evaluates
dozens of heuristics head-to-head, and the iterated greedy of Ruiz and
St\"{u}tzle~\citep{ruizstutzle07} demonstrated that a simple
destruction--construction loop matches far more elaborate machinery.

Two ingredients recur in every strong PFSP algorithm: a carefully
chosen move structure, and the critical-path properties that prune it.
Both have become objects of study in their own right. Dynasearch,
introduced by Congram, Potts and van de Velde for single-machine
scheduling~\citep{congram02}, showed that an exponentially large set
of pairwise independent moves can be searched exactly by dynamic
programming in polynomial time --- a compound move, rather than a
single swap, becomes the unit of search. Compound moves grew a theory
of their own: Bo\.{z}ejko and Wodecki analyzed the properties of swap
multimoves~\citep{bozejko2007}, and the paradigm broadened into very
large-scale neighborhood search~\citep{ahuja2002}. The block properties of the
critical path~\citep{nowicki96,ref_smutnicki,grabowski04} attack the
same bottleneck from the other side, telling the search which
candidates cannot improve the makespan and can be skipped wholesale.
The Fibonacci and Motzkin neighborhoods we introduced in earlier
work~\citep{WojewodzkiBozejkoBPASTS2026,Motzkin_PWR_EXIT} continue the
compound-move line: their move sets are counted by classical integer
sequences, searched by dynamic programming --- and, as this paper
exploits, expressible as QUBOs. Motzkin is the richest member of the
family: its admissible moves are the non-crossing, arbitrarily nested
arc systems counted by the Motzkin numbers, which makes it both the
most expressive neighborhood in this study and, as the experiments
will show, the sharpest probe of what a decomposition destroys.

Quantum optimization entered scheduling from the job shop side.
Venturelli et al.\ encoded whole job shop instances as QUBOs for
quantum annealing~\citep{venturelli16}, Kurowski et al.\ hybridized
the annealer with classical heuristics~\citep{kurowski20}, and Amaro
et al.\ studied variational gate-based circuits on small
instances~\citep{amaro22}. These formulations encode the entire
schedule as one QUBO, which caps them at toy sizes on current
hardware. The alternative explored in our line of work is to put the
QPU \emph{inside} the metaheuristic rather than in place of it: the
neighborhood, not the schedule, becomes the QUBO.

In~\citep{WojewodzkiBozejkoBPASTS2026} we formulated four classical PFSP
neighborhoods as Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO)
problems and executed them on the D-Wave quantum annealing QPU, providing
the first systematic comparison of classical and quantum neighborhood
search under identical wall-clock time budgets and metaheuristic frameworks;
hybrid metaheuristic strategies combining several neighborhoods were
investigated in~\citep{WojewodzkiBozejko2026}.
The experimental scope was restricted to Taillard instances with $n = 20$
jobs, imposed jointly by the variable-count constraints of the QUBO
formulations and the effective embedding capacity of the D-Wave
\texttt{Advantage\_system4.1} QPU (approximately 170--180 logical variables
for dense QUBO graphs on the Pegasus P16 topology with 5{,}627 active qubits).

This paper addresses both restrictions directly. Two properties of the
original formulations block scalability. The first is an unnecessary
delta filter: the original implementation discarded swap candidates
with non-negative makespan delta ($\delta_k \geq 0$) before QUBO
assembly. That filter was a variable-count reduction for the classical
simulated-annealing sampler (dimod's \texttt{SimulatedAnnealingSampler})
used to prototype the QUBOs --- harmless to that solver's optimum, but
counterproductive on real QPU hardware, where the QPU assigns
$x_k = 0$ to non-improving candidates on its own while the filter
discards information about the joint penalty structure that helps the
annealer avoid degenerate solutions. For Adjacent and Fibonacci, whose
variable count is $K = n - 1$ regardless of filtering, removing it
costs nothing in embedding overhead. The second blocker is the
variable-count ceiling of the $O(n^2)$ neighborhoods: Dynasearch and
Motzkin exceed QPU capacity for $n > 20$. Here we introduce a windowed
decomposition that partitions the permutation into overlapping windows
of size $w \leq 19$, solves a QUBO per window, and merges the results
by greedy conflict resolution. Together these changes give twelve
neighborhoods in three classes --- classical, quantum QUBO, and
quantum QUBO enhanced --- covering the full Taillard benchmark range
for the first time.

\paragraph{Contributions}
The paper contributes, first, the windowed QUBO decomposition for the
Dynasearch and Motzkin neighborhoods, which makes QPU execution
feasible up to $n = 50$ with the window size derived automatically
from hardware capacity, and, second, delta-filter-free formulations
that carry Adjacent to $n \leq 200$ and Fibonacci to $n \leq 500$ on
the D-Wave Advantage QPU. All twelve neighborhoods share the block-property
accelerator of Smutnicki~\citep{ref_smutnicki}, which cuts the QUBO
variable count by 30--60\% before submission. On the metaheuristic
side, ILS replaces uniform random restarts with the BackTrackJumpList:
double-bridge perturbations of the $k = 10$ best solutions seen during
the search. The experimental study is, to our knowledge, the first
comparison of all three neighborhood classes across
$n \in \{20, 50, 100, 200, 500\}$ under identical metaheuristic
frameworks and time budgets. Throughout, the Motzkin neighborhood acts
as the touchstone of the study: it wins the single-move regime on the
QPU, and its globally nested structure is precisely what makes
windowed decomposition costly --- the structural asymmetry this paper
quantifies.

\section{Problem Definition}
\label{sec:problem}

This section fixes the model, the notation, and the two computational
tools used throughout the paper. All of it is standard and well known;
we recall it briefly, in the notation of the classical tabu search
paper of Nowicki and Smutnicki~\citep{nowicki96}, to keep the paper
self-contained.

\paragraph{Permutation Flow Shop Problem}
The flow shop consists of a set of
jobs $J = \{1,\ldots,n\}$ and a set of machines
$M = \{1,\ldots,m\}$. Job $j \in J$ is a sequence of $m$ operations
$O_{1j}, O_{2j}, \ldots, O_{mj}$; operation $O_{ij}$ has to be
processed on machine $i$ for an uninterrupted time $p_{ij} > 0$. Each
machine can process at most one job at a time, each job can be
processed on at most one machine at a time, and every machine
processes the jobs in the same order, so a processing order is a
permutation $\pi = (\pi(1), \ldots, \pi(n))$ of the set $J$; $\Pi$
denotes the set of all such permutations. Completion times satisfy
the recursion
\begin{equation}
  C_{i,\pi(j)} = \max\!\bigl\{C_{i-1,\pi(j)},\, C_{i,\pi(j-1)}\bigr\}
  + p_{i,\pi(j)}, \qquad i \in M,\; j \in J,
\end{equation}
with $C_{0,\pi(j)} = C_{i,\pi(0)} = 0$. The makespan of $\pi$ is
$C_{\max}(\pi) = C_{m,\pi(n)}$, and the problem is to find
$\pi^* \in \Pi$ such that
$C_{\max}(\pi^*) = \min_{\pi \in \Pi} C_{\max}(\pi)$.

Equivalently, the makespan is the length of a longest (critical) path
in the grid graph of the schedule,
\begin{equation}
  C_{\max}(\pi) = \max_{1 = j_0 \leq j_1 \leq \cdots \leq j_m = n}\;
  \sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=j_{i-1}}^{j_i} p_{i,\pi(j)},
  \label{eq:critpath}
\end{equation}
and the maximal subsequences of a critical path processed on a single
machine form its \emph{blocks} --- the structure behind the block
properties that Section~\ref{sec:accelerators} turns into a candidate
filter. The complete treatment of the model, its critical-path and
block structure, and the tabu search built on them is given
in~\citep{nowicki96} and the scheduling
monograph~\citep{smutnicki2012}; relations between permutations also
admit an algebraic treatment via positive definite functions on
permutation groups~\citep{bozejko2015}, though we do not need that
machinery here. We recall only what the neighborhood and QUBO
constructions require.

\paragraph{Head--Tail decomposition}
Following~\citep{wodecki04}, the makespan delta of a candidate move is
computed in $O(1)$ using precomputed head matrices $H[i][j]$ (earliest
completion time of the job at position $j$ on machine $i$, scanning
from the left) and tail matrices $T[i][j]$ (latest completion-time
contribution, scanning from the right). Both matrices are built in
$O(mn)$ before each local search iteration and reused for all
candidate evaluations within that iteration.

\paragraph{Metaheuristics}
A neighborhood by itself is not yet a search algorithm: it needs a
control layer that accepts or rejects the proposed moves and pulls the
trajectory out of local optima. To keep that layer from confounding
the comparison, every neighborhood in this study runs inside the same
two frameworks with identical parameters and time budgets, so the
neighborhood is the only component that varies.

\emph{Iterated Local Search} (ILS)~\citep{lourenco2003iterated} runs
with a tabu list~\citep{glover1989} of tenure $\tau = 10$ and an
aspiration criterion that accepts a tabu move if it strictly improves
the incumbent best $C_{\max}^*$ (the lowest makespan the search has
reached so far, as opposed to the external reference optimum $C^*$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:rpd}), in the spirit of the fast taboo search that
Nowicki and Smutnicki developed for the job shop~\citep{nowicki96ms}.
When no non-tabu move is available and aspiration is not met, the
search diversifies via the BackTrackJumpList mechanism described in
Section~\ref{sec:btjl}.

\emph{Simulated Annealing} (SA)~\citep{kirkpatrick1983} runs with
initial temperature $T_0 = 1000$, cooling rate $\alpha = 0.99$,
temperature floor $T_{\min} = 100$, and reheat-kick diversification on
stagnation (Section~\ref{sec:sa-kick}). Our variant differs from canonical SA in
its \emph{exploration criterion}, the component that decides which
neighbor faces the Metropolis test~\citep{franzin19}. Canonical SA
samples a random neighbor; Ishibuchi et al.\ proposed, for the flow
shop specifically, submitting the best of a random \emph{sample} of
neighbors, and showed that this makes SA robust to the choice of the
cooling schedule~\citep{ishibuchi95}. We take that mechanism to its
exhaustive limit: each step receives the best move of the
\emph{entire} neighborhood from the shared oracle --- the same oracle
ILS uses --- and the Metropolis criterion decides whether to accept
it. The limit is not innocent: with a random sample, a rejection is
followed by a fresh sample, whereas a deterministic full scan
re-proposes the same move at an ever lower temperature, which is
exactly the freezing pathology that Section~\ref{sec:sa-kick}
diagnoses and the reheat-kick resolves. The design is forced by the
comparison itself: a QPU call returns the best composite move, not a
random one, so the classical control layer must consume a best-move
oracle for the twelve neighborhoods to remain interchangeable.
Full-neighborhood evaluation under Metropolis-type acceptance is
itself an established regime in QUBO hardware --- Fujitsu's Digital
Annealer tests every single-flip neighbor in parallel at each
sweep~\citep{aramon19} --- and in the rejection-free Monte Carlo
family, which scans all neighbors but samples the successor from the
acceptance weights instead of taking the argmax~\citep{rosenthal21}.
To our knowledge, the specific chain used here --- exhaustive argmax
proposal under Metropolis acceptance --- has not been named or
analyzed in the literature; Table~\ref{tab:rpd_n20} and
Section~\ref{sec:sa-kick} document its behavior empirically.

\subsection{BackTrackJumpList Elite Diversification}
\label{sec:btjl}

Uniform random restarts reinitialize from an arbitrary point and throw
away everything learned up to that moment. The BackTrackJumpList keeps
that information.

It stores the $k = 10$ best distinct permutations seen during the run,
sorted by $C_{\max}$.
When a tabu conflict occurs and the aspiration criterion is not met,
the algorithm applies a \emph{double-bridge} (4-opt) perturbation to
the next elite in the pool (round-robin order) instead of sampling
a new random permutation.

A double-bridge move cuts $\pi$ at three uniformly sampled positions
$a < b < c$ into segments
$A = \pi_{[1,a)}$, $B = \pi_{[a,b)}$, $C = \pi_{[b,c)}$, $D = \pi_{[c,n]}$
and reconnects them as $A \mid C \mid B \mid D$.
No single adjacent swap can reverse this; it lies outside the 2-opt
neighborhood, so the search genuinely escapes the current basin.
Round-robin selection spreads diversification events across all elites
rather than hammering the global best repeatedly.
The pool is updated after every strict improvement to $C_{\max}^*$;
before any elites have been found, the fallback is a uniform random restart.

\subsection{SA with reheat-kick diversification}
\label{sec:sa-kick}

The shared neighborhood oracles force SA's inner step to consume
the \emph{best} candidate move, not a random one. With a
deterministic best-move oracle, SA collapses at a local minimum
$X$: the oracle returns the best neighbor $X'$ with $\Delta =
C_{\max}(X') - C_{\max}(X) > 0$; Metropolis accepts $X'$ with
probability $\exp(-\Delta/T)$; from $X'$ the oracle returns $X$
again with $\Delta < 0$, accepted unconditionally. The trajectory
settles into a two-cycle $X \leftrightarrow X'$, and reheating
cannot break it: the oracle yields the same pair regardless of $T$.

The fix is to couple reheating with a perturbation in solution
space, similar to the BackTrackJumpList of Section~\ref{sec:btjl}.
SA keeps an elite pool of size $k = 10$, updated whenever
$C_{\max}^*$ strictly improves. On stagnation, defined as $s =
100$\,ms without a global improvement, the algorithm:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item resets the temperature to its initial value, $T \leftarrow
        T_0$. A multiplicative reheat $T \leftarrow T \cdot r$ would
        be useless here: the schedule $T_{k+1} = T_k \cdot \alpha$
        drives $T$ to the floor $T_{\min}$ in tens of milliseconds,
        and from such a small $T$ the factor $r$ adds nothing;
  \item replaces the current solution with a double-bridge
        perturbation of the next elite (round-robin), placing the
        search outside the current basin of attraction;
  \item resets the stagnation timer.
\end{enumerate}
The temperature floor $T_{\min} = 100$ keeps some uphill mobility
during cooling, so post-kick steps stay exploratory until the next
reheat fires. The two-cycle disappears, and SA converges with
seed-dependent trajectories on instances where the original
schedule had locked onto the first local minimum.

\section{Neighborhood Taxonomy}
\label{sec:neighborhoods}

This section walks through the twelve neighborhoods in their three
classes: the four classical neighborhoods, the shared block-property
accelerator, their quantum QUBO formulations, and the enhanced
variants this paper introduces. The classical definitions are well
known from the literature and our earlier work; we recall them because
the QUBO constructions of Section~\ref{sec:qubo} are built directly on
their move sets.

\subsection{Classical Neighborhoods}

The four classical neighborhoods enumerate candidate job-insertion
moves and evaluate each via the Head--Tail decomposition.

\paragraph{Adjacent \textup{($N_{\mathrm{adj}}$)}}
The move swaps the jobs at positions $i$ and $i+1$ for
$i \in \{1,\ldots,n-1\}$, so the neighborhood has cardinality
$|N_{\mathrm{adj}}| = n-1$.
In the scheduling literature this move is known as the adjacent
pairwise interchange (API); Della Croce's generalized pairwise
interchanges~\citep{dellacroce95} place it at the bottom of a whole
hierarchy of interchange moves.

\paragraph{Fibonacci \textup{($N_{\mathrm{fib}}$)}}
The move applies a set of non-overlapping adjacent swaps
simultaneously: a subset $S \subseteq \{1,\ldots,n-1\}$ is valid if
$i \in S \Rightarrow i+1 \notin S$.
The number of such valid subsets in a permutation of length $n$ equals the
Fibonacci number $F_{n+1}$, which gives the neighborhood its
name; we introduced it in~\citep{WojewodzkiBozejko2026}.
The optimal subset minimizing the joint makespan change is selected by
dynamic programming in $O(n)$ time given precomputed adjacent-swap deltas.

\paragraph{Dynasearch \textup{($N_{\mathrm{dyn}}$)}}
The neighborhood consists of endpoint-swap moves over segments of
arbitrary length: for every pair $(i,j)$ with $i < j \leq n-1$ the move exchanges
$\pi(i)$ and $\pi(j)$, leaving the interior $\pi(i+1),\ldots,\pi(j-1)$
unchanged. Dynamic programming~\citep{congram02} selects the subset
of pairs that are pairwise non-overlapping as intervals
($j_1 < i_2$ or $j_2 < i_1$) and jointly minimize the resulting
$C_{\max}$. The candidate set has cardinality $K = O(n^2)$ and the DP
runs in $O(n^3)$ in the worst case, accelerated by the NPI block-property
filter described in Section~\ref{sec:accelerators}.

\paragraph{Motzkin \textup{($N_{\mathrm{motz}}$)}}
The moves are the same endpoint swaps as in Dynasearch (pairs $(i,j)$
with $i < j \leq n-1$, exchanging only $\pi(i)$ and $\pi(j)$), but the
admissible subsets follow Motzkin path
combinatorics~\citep{motzkin1948,Motzkin_PWR,Motzkin_PWR_EXIT}: pairs may not cross
($i_1 < i_2 < j_1 < j_2$ is forbidden) nor share an endpoint, while
strict nesting $i_1 < i_2 < j_2 < j_1$ is allowed. The number of
admissible pair subsets equals the Motzkin number
$M_n = \sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}\binom{n}{2k}C_k$, where $C_k$
is the $k$-th Catalan number
($M_5 = 21$, $M_{10} = 4\,862$, $M_{20} \approx 1.7 \times 10^{10}$).
The DP runs in $O(n^3)$, with the same $K = O(n^2)$ candidate set as
Dynasearch.

\subsection{Block-Property Accelerators}
\label{sec:accelerators}

All twelve neighborhoods apply the block-property accelerator of
Smutnicki~\citep{ref_smutnicki} before evaluating or assembling QUBO
variables. The accelerator is derived from the following structural
observation: a position $u \in \{1,\ldots,n-1\}$ is a
\emph{block boundary} if the last machine $m$ is busy immediately
before processing job $\pi(u+1)$, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
  H[m][u] = H[m-1][u] + p_{m,\pi(u+1)}.
  \label{eq:boundary}
\end{equation}
The complete set of block boundaries $\mathcal{U}(\pi) \subseteq
\{1,\ldots,n-1\}$ is identified in $O(mn)$ from the Head matrix
already computed for delta evaluation.

The key theorem~\citep{ref_smutnicki} states: \emph{swapping jobs at
positions $i$ and $j$} \textup{(with $i < j$)} \emph{can strictly decrease $C_{\max}$
only if $\mathcal{U}(\pi) \cap [i, j] \neq \emptyset$}, i.e., at
least one block boundary lies within the span of the swap.

\paragraph{Accelerator for adjacent and Fibonacci neighborhoods}
Swapping adjacent positions $(i, i+1)$ improves $C_{\max}$ only if
$i \in \mathcal{U}(\pi)$. This filters the $n-1$ candidate swaps of
$N_{\mathrm{adj}}$ down to $|\mathcal{U}(\pi)|$ candidates, which
equals the number of blocks minus one (typically $O(m)$ on
well-structured instances). On Taillard benchmarks with $m = 5$
and $n = 100$, this reduces the evaluation count from $99$ to
$4$--$8$ on average. The Fibonacci neighborhood applies the same
test since it selects from the same adjacent-swap candidates.

\paragraph{NPI accelerator for endpoint-swap neighborhoods}
NPI stands for non-adjacent pairwise interchange, the general
interchange move of~\citep{dellacroce95}.
For Dynasearch and Motzkin, where candidate pairs $(i, j)$ span
arbitrary distances, the span filter $\mathcal{U}(\pi) \cap [i,j] \neq \emptyset$
reduces the $O(n^2)$ candidate set to at most $O(n \cdot |\mathcal{U}|)$
pairs. In the quantum QUBO formulations, the filter is applied before
QUBO assembly: only filtered pairs become QUBO variables, directly
reducing the embedding overhead on the QPU.
On Taillard instances, this filter eliminates 35--65\% of candidate
pairs across the full size range tested.

Block boundaries are computed once per iteration (reusing the Head
matrix) and shared across all candidate evaluations and QUBO
constructions within that iteration.

\subsection{Quantum QUBO Neighborhoods}

The quantum QUBO neighborhoods reformulate the four classical
neighborhood searches as QUBO problems solved on the D-Wave Advantage QPU
hardware via the \texttt{EmbeddingComposite} sampler. The general
form follows~\citep{WojewodzkiBozejkoBPASTS2026}: binary variable $x_k = 1$
indicates that swap candidate $k$ is selected; the QUBO encodes both
the objective (minimize $\sum_k \delta_k x_k$) and the feasibility
constraints (at most one swap selected, or non-conflicting swaps for
composite moves). These formulations are feasible for $n \leq 20$ and
are described in detail in~\citep{WojewodzkiBozejkoBPASTS2026}.

\subsection{Quantum QUBO Enhanced Neighborhoods}
\label{sec:enhanced}

\paragraph{Full QUBO without delta filtering \textup{(Adjacent, Fibonacci Enhanced)}}
The original formulations filtered candidates with $\delta_k \geq 0$
before QUBO construction to reduce the variable count for the
classical SimulatedAnnealingSampler used in testing. On actual QPU
hardware this filter is counterproductive: the QPU natively assigns
$x_k = 0$ to non-improving candidates without any variable-count
reduction, while the filter removes information about the joint
penalty structure. Retaining all $K = n - 1$ variables produces a
QUBO that is simultaneously more informative and no larger.
Since $K = n - 1$ grows linearly in $n$, these formulations remain
within QPU capacity for $n \leq 200$ (Adjacent Enhanced, dense $Q$)
and $n \leq 500$ (Fibonacci Enhanced, tridiagonal $Q$).

\paragraph{Windowed QUBO decomposition \textup{(Dynasearch, Motzkin Enhanced)}}
For Dynasearch and Motzkin, the full QUBO has $K = O(n^2)$ variables,
exceeding QPU capacity for $n > 20$. The windowed decomposition
partitions the permutation into overlapping windows: window $\ell$
covers positions $[s_\ell,\, s_\ell + w)$, where $s_\ell = \ell \cdot
\lfloor w(1 - \rho) \rfloor$ and $\rho = 0.5$ is the overlap ratio.
For each window a QUBO is assembled over the
$\binom{w}{2}$ candidate pairs $(i,j)$ with $s_\ell \leq i < j < s_\ell + w$
(further filtered by the block-property accelerator).
The window size is chosen automatically to satisfy
$K(w) = w(w-1)/2 \leq C_{\mathrm{eff}}$, where $C_{\mathrm{eff}} = 180$
is a conservative estimate of the D-Wave Advantage QPU effective capacity for
dense QUBOs:
\begin{equation}
  w_{\max} = \left\lfloor
    \frac{1 + \sqrt{1 + 8\,C_{\mathrm{eff}}}}{2}
  \right\rfloor = 19.
  \label{eq:window}
\end{equation}
Individual window QUBOs are submitted sequentially; solutions are merged
by accepting the best move from each window and resolving conflicts
(overlapping pairs) greedily, preferring moves with larger
$|\delta_k|$. This decomposition enables Dynasearch Enhanced and
Motzkin Enhanced to operate on instances up to $n = 50$.

The tractability analysis for all twelve neighborhoods is summarized
in Table~\ref{tab:feasibility}.

\section{QUBO Formulations}
\label{sec:qubo}

All quantum neighborhoods share the general QUBO form
(see~\citep{lucas2014ising} for a survey of Ising/QUBO formulations
of NP problems):
\begin{equation}
  \min_{\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^K}\;\mathbf{x}^\top Q\,\mathbf{x}
  \;\xrightarrow{x_i=(1-Z_i)/2}\;
  H_C = \sum_i h_i Z_i + \sum_{i<j} J_{ij} Z_i Z_j.
  \label{eq:qubo}
\end{equation}
Diagonal entries $Q_{kk} = \delta_k$ encode the makespan change from
swap $k$ evaluated by the Head--Tail technique. Off-diagonal entries
$Q_{kl} = \lambda$ penalize conflicting swap pairs with penalty
$\lambda = \bigl(\sum_k |\delta_k|\bigr) + 1$, which dominates the
objective and ensures feasibility.

\paragraph{Adjacent Enhanced}
The one-hot constraint sets $Q_{ii} = \delta_i$ and $Q_{ij} = 2\lambda$
for all $i \neq j$, so exactly one swap is selected.

\paragraph{Fibonacci Enhanced}
The QUBO is tridiagonal: $Q_{ii} = \delta_i$ and
$Q_{i,i+1} = \lambda$.
The number of feasible (independent set) solutions equals $F_{n+1}$
($(n+1)$-th Fibonacci number), matching the name of the neighborhood.

\paragraph{Dynasearch Enhanced}
$Q_{kl} = \lambda$ iff pairs $(i_1,j_1)$ and $(i_2,j_2)$ overlap:
$\max(i_1,i_2) \leq \min(j_1,j_2)$. Non-overlapping pairs are
independent ($Q_{kl} = 0$), allowing simultaneous multi-swap moves.

\paragraph{Motzkin Enhanced}
$Q_{kl} = \lambda$ iff arcs $(i_1,j_1)$ and $(i_2,j_2)$ conflict
under Motzkin rules (shared endpoint or crossing arcs).
Nested and disjoint arcs are unconstrained.
The feasible solution count equals $M_n$~\citep{Motzkin_PWR}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The design holds every component fixed except the neighborhood:
benchmark instances, seeds, time budgets, hardware, and metrics are
shared by all twelve variants, so differences in the tables that
follow are attributable to the move structure alone.

\paragraph{Benchmarks}
We use the standard Taillard benchmark
instances~\citep{ref_taillard}: $n \in \{20, 50, 100, 200, 500\}$
jobs and $m \in \{5, 10, 20\}$ machines.
For each $(n, m)$ configuration, 10 instances are used (3 configurations
per size class, 150 instances total).
Each algorithm runs with 5 independent seeds per instance; the seed
determines only the initial random permutation and the SA temperature
schedule.
We test six time budgets, $t_{\max}$ from 100\,ms to 10\,000\,ms
(100, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000, and 10\,000). Classical neighborhoods run at all six; the
QPU campaigns report $t_{\max} = 1000$ and $5000$\,ms in the tables,
and the convergence analysis of Fig.~\ref{fig:qpu_conv_n20} covers
100--5000\,ms for the enhanced class.

\paragraph{Hardware}
Classical neighborhoods run on an Apple M4 Pro (12 cores, 24\,GB RAM).
Quantum QUBO and Enhanced neighborhoods use the \textbf{D-Wave
\texttt{Advantage\_system4.1}} QPU (Pegasus P16 topology, 5{,}627 active
physical qubits out of 5{,}760 fabricated, 40{,}279 couplers), accessed
via the Leap cloud platform with \texttt{EmbeddingComposite} and
\texttt{num\_reads\,=\,100} (per window for Enhanced neighborhoods).
The number of \emph{logical} qubits available for a given QUBO depends on
the graph structure after minor-embedding: for dense conflict graphs
the practical capacity is roughly 190--200 logical variables (each
logical qubit maps to a chain of $\sim$30 physical qubits). The bound
is soft --- the dense $K_{199}$ QUBO of adjacent enhanced at $n = 200$
embedded in all 50 runs, while dynasearch's $\sim$190-variable QUBO
failed occasionally, and those failures succeeded on retry --- whereas
chain-structured graphs (Fibonacci) embed trivially up to $n \leq 500$,
as their near-linear variable count requires short embedding chains.
The exact solver identity (\texttt{Advantage\_system4.1}) was confirmed
programmatically via \texttt{DWaveSampler().solver.id} at experiment time.
Classical experiments use \texttt{SimulatedAnnealingSampler} from
\texttt{dimod} for QUBO evaluation.

\paragraph{Metrics}
We report solution quality as Relative Percentage Deviation (RPD)
from the best-known Taillard makespan $C^*$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RPD} = \frac{C_{\max}(\pi) - C^*}{C^*} \times 100\%.
  \label{eq:rpd}
\end{equation}
We assess statistical significance with the two-sided Wilcoxon
signed-rank test ($\alpha = 0.05$) between pairs of neighborhood variants.

\paragraph{Instance constraints}
Following the tractability analysis of Table~\ref{tab:feasibility}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Quantum QUBO (original): $n = 20$ only.
  \item Quantum Adjacent Enhanced: $n \leq 200$.
  \item Quantum Fibonacci Enhanced: $n \leq 500$.
  \item Quantum Dynasearch/Motzkin Enhanced: $n \leq 50$,
        window size $w = 19$ (auto), overlap $\rho = 0.5$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Comparison at $n = 20$}
In the classical baseline (Tables~\ref{tab:rpd_n20},
\ref{tab:rpd_n20_full}) dynasearch and motzkin lead adjacent and
fibonacci at every $m$, and the lead grows with $m$ (0.7--1.2 pp at
$m = 5$, 1.2--1.4 at $m = 20$). The SA gap to ILS is neighborhood
dependent: SA adjacent and fibonacci trail by 7--10 pp at $m = 5$,
because a best-move oracle over a sparse neighborhood traps SA in
narrow basins that the reheat-kick only partly resolves, whereas SA
dynasearch and motzkin stay within 0.7 pp of ILS --- their richer
move sets supply enough escape paths.

\paragraph{Quantum QUBO on the QPU at $n = 20$}
Every quantum run records its own iteration count, so the comparison
regime is explicit. At $t_{\max} = 1000$\,ms each run completes exactly
one metaheuristic iteration --- one QPU-selected composite move on the
initial solution --- because a single call costs about 2.1\,s of wall
time for adjacent and fibonacci, more for the others. In this regime
the ranking inverts relative to the classical rows: motzkin leads at
21.7\,\%, then fibonacci (23.8\,\%), adjacent (25.1\,\%), and
dynasearch (26.9\,\%) --- when one move is all the budget allows, the
richest admissible move set wins, and motzkin's non-crossing arcs are
the richest of the four. Quality then tracks the iteration count
(Fig.~\ref{fig:qpu_conv_n20}): at $t_{\max} = 5000$\,ms adjacent and
fibonacci fit three iterations and improve by 2.8 and 3.7\,pp
(fibonacci to 19.9\,\%, motzkin to 19.5\,\%), while dynasearch stays
at one iteration and moves half a point.
Table~\ref{tab:rpd_n20_tl} gives the same comparison per budget without
ILS/SA pooling: the flat enhanced rows below 1000\,ms are this
single-iteration regime.

Dynasearch also exposes the embedding bound: its unfiltered QUBO
carries roughly 190 densely coupled variables and 4 of 200 runs found
no embedding, whereas motzkin --- same variable count but a sparser
conflict graph, since nesting adds no penalty edges --- embedded every
time. QPU feasibility is governed by conflict-graph density, not
variable count alone.

The enhanced rows of Table~\ref{tab:rpd_n20} give the controlled
measurement this design promised, and the outcome splits by
neighborhood structure. For the linear neighborhoods the enhanced
variants reproduce the originals almost exactly (adjacent 25.04 vs.\
25.05, fibonacci 23.60/23.66 vs.\ 23.79/23.46; Wilcoxon signed-rank
over instance$\times$seed pairs, $p \geq 0.25$): their QUBOs were never
delta-filtered to begin with, so the agreement doubles as a
between-campaign replication check. Windowed dynasearch \emph{gains}
0.9\,pp over the full formulation (25.78 vs.\ 26.64 paired,
$p = 0.012$) while eliminating embedding failures entirely --- zero
across 400 enhanced runs, against a 4\% failure rate for the full
$\sim$190-variable QUBO. Windowed motzkin \emph{loses} 4.1\,pp
(25.72 vs.\ 21.58, $p < 10^{-4}$). The asymmetry
has a structural reading: dynasearch conflicts are local (overlapping
segments), so a window boundary discards little, whereas motzkin's
strength lies in nested arc systems that span the permutation, and
windows cut exactly those. The budget dimension sharpens the picture.
At $t_{\max} = 5000$\,ms windowed dynasearch keeps its edge (25.07 vs.\
26.15, $p = 0.005$), but the motzkin gap widens from 4.1 to 5.6\,pp
(25.06 vs.\ 19.45, $p < 10^{-4}$): the full formulation converts every
additional iteration into
nesting gains that no within-window move can express, so the windowing
loss compounds rather than amortizes. Windowed decomposition is close
to free where move interactions are local and costs real solution
quality --- increasingly with the time budget --- where they are
global.

\paragraph{Scaling behavior}
Table~\ref{tab:rpd_scaling} shows how enhanced quantum neighborhoods
compare to classical counterparts across sizes. The enhanced variants
were built so that QPU evaluation of larger QUBOs, windowed where
necessary, would at least hold its ground as $n$ grows. The
measurements show more: quantum RPD falls monotonically with
instance size. Fibonacci enhanced runs 31.7--23.6--19.6--15.0\,\%
from $n = 20$ to $n = 200$, and adjacent enhanced tracks the same
slope about 1.5\,pp behind (34.9 down to 16.4\,\%). The gap to the
classical fibonacci baseline narrows from 20.4\,pp at $n = 20$ to
13.5 at $n = 100$, 8.8 at $n = 200$, and 4.6 at $n = 500$, where the
quantum neighborhood (15.75\,\%) reaches parity with classical
adjacent (15.36\,\%). Fibonacci's tridiagonal QUBO is what makes the
right end of that curve reachable at all: it embeds in milliseconds
even at $K_{499}$, so three metaheuristic iterations per run fit the
5\,s budget at every size. Adjacent drops to a single iteration from
$n = 100$ on, where minor-embedding search for its dense QUBO starts
to eat the budget.

The $n = 20$ column replicates the windowing asymmetry of
Table~\ref{tab:rpd_n20} at $m = 10$: windowed dynasearch again edges
out its full formulation (38.6 vs.\ 39.3\,\%, though at 50 pairs the
difference does not reach significance, $p = 0.18$), and windowed
motzkin again pays about 4\,pp (38.4 vs.\ 34.4\,\%, $p < 10^{-4}$).

The dense one-hot QUBO also scales further than the $n = 20$ campaign
suggested. Adjacent enhanced at $n = 200$ carries $K_{199}$ --- just
past the $\sim$190-variable capacity estimate derived from
dynasearch's failures --- yet all 50 runs embedded, at about 8\,s of
minorminer search per call. The $n = 20$ failures themselves turned
out to be stochastic rather than structural: the three dynasearch
runs that found no embedding on the first pass in this campaign all
succeeded on a retry. The hard wall sits at $K_{499}$ (adjacent,
$n = 500$), which exceeds the Pegasus clique capacity outright;
around 200 variables, embedding is a lottery with favorable odds,
not a cliff.

At $n = 100$, dynasearch still wins: 4.67\,\% against fibonacci's
6.15\,\% ($m = 10$, $t_{\max} = 5$\,s). At $n = 200$ that reverses.
Fibonacci holds at 6.19\,\% while dynasearch jumps to 11.63\,\%.
The reason is the same as the motzkin collapse at $n = 50$: the
$O(n^2)$ dynasearch DP, already non-trivial at $n = 100$, eats enough
wall time at $n = 200$ that ILS completes fewer full scans and
accumulates fewer improvements within the budget. Fibonacci's $O(n)$
evaluation does not have that problem.

Motzkin fares worse still, from a 0.2\,pp deficit to dynasearch at
$n = 20$ to last place at $n = 100$ (14.21\,\%): its $O(n^3)$
arc-selection DP is costlier per call than dynasearch's $O(n^2)$, so
ILS runs fewer iterations. The classical crossover therefore sits
between $n = 100$ and $n = 200$ --- dynasearch competitive up to
$n = 100$, fibonacci the right baseline beyond, reaching 11.12\,\%
against adjacent's 15.36\,\% at $n = 500$.

At $n = 500$ both dynasearch and motzkin run out of room. A single
dynasearch DP call (over $\approx 1.2 \times 10^5$ candidate pairs)
takes about 200\,s; motzkin's $O(n^3)$ arc-selection DP takes about
30\,s. Both exceed every tested budget, so ILS and SA each complete
exactly one local-search iteration per run and return identical
solutions. Dynasearch ends up at 14.53\,\% (range $13.30$--$16.05$),
motzkin at 16.26\,\% (range $13.00$--$18.40$); these document the
scaling boundary rather than a fair head-to-head. Even so,
dynasearch's single call still beats adjacent's thousands of
iterations (15.36\,\%): one call picks a near-optimal set of
non-overlapping swaps and applies them at once, whereas adjacent's
per-iteration gains shrink fast. At this scale the move structure
matters more than the move count, as long as the move carries enough
swaps.

The windowed dynasearch and motzkin variants stop at $n = 50$, where
they land within 0.4\,pp of each other (26.37 and 26.03\,\%) and
about 3\,pp behind fibonacci enhanced (23.56\,\%). Their bound is
wall time, not embedding: every window embeds reliably, but at
$n = 50$ a single dynasearch call already spends minutes of
minorminer search across its $\sim$5 windows, and the $O(n^3)$
motzkin QUBO build grows just as fast. Past $n = 50$ the per-call
cost leaves no room for a single iteration inside any practical
budget --- the same wall-time wall that removed classical dynasearch
and motzkin at $n = 500$, reached two size classes earlier. For QPU
experiments beyond $n = 200$, fibonacci enhanced is the only quantum
neighborhood that still scales without truncation.

\paragraph{Windowed decomposition quality}
The approximation the windowing introduces --- swap pairs crossing
window boundaries are never evaluated --- is what the $n = 20$ loss
above quantifies. All runs use overlap ratio $\rho = 0.5$; larger
overlap would reduce boundary artifacts at the cost of more QPU
submissions per iteration, and sensitivity to $\rho$ remains untested.

\paragraph{BackTrackJumpList contribution}
A controlled ablation on the classical neighborhoods (3600 paired
runs: both arms at $n = 20$, $m \in \{5, 10, 20\}$,
$t_{\max} \in \{1, 5, 10\}$\,s, 10 instances $\times$ 5 seeds) isolates
the elite-pool effect. ILS with the BackTrackJumpList beats ILS with
uniform random restart in every machine-count/budget cell, by
1.1--2.6\,pp of mean RPD (Wilcoxon signed-rank, $p < 10^{-17}$ per
cell). The margin tracks neighborhood richness: sparse neighborhoods,
which rely on diversification to escape narrow basins, gain 4.1\,pp
(adjacent) and 3.5\,pp (fibonacci), while dynasearch and motzkin ---
whose composite moves supply their own escape paths --- gain a small
but significant 0.4 and 0.3\,pp ($p < 10^{-19}$).

\section{Conclusions}

The quantum QUBO enhanced class breaks the $n = 20$ barrier for
quantum annealing-based PFSP search through two changes: removing the
delta filter for linear-variable neighborhoods and applying a windowed
decomposition for quadratic-variable ones, with the Smutnicki
accelerator cutting variable counts by 30--60\% and the
BackTrackJumpList replacing random restarts in ILS.

The July 2026 QPU campaigns filled every tractable cell of
Tables~\ref{tab:rpd_n20} and~\ref{tab:rpd_scaling} with real
\texttt{Advantage\_system4.1} measurements and answered the two
hypotheses the design posed. Fibonacci Enhanced holds its ground
across the full range, its gap to the classical baseline narrowing
from 20.4\,pp at $n = 20$ to 4.6 at $n = 500$. The windowed
decomposition's cost is structural, not uniform: it is near-free for
dynasearch, whose conflicts are local, but grows with the budget for
motzkin, whose strength is the cross-window nesting the windows cut.
Throughout, the single-iteration regime, the QPU call-cost anatomy,
and the stochasticity of embedding near the capacity bound are
reported alongside the quality numbers because they define what a fair
wall-clock comparison of quantum and classical neighborhood search can
claim.

\paragraph{Future work}
Future work will pursue adaptive window sizing driven by per-window
convergence feedback, QAOA circuits on gate-based
processors~\citep{ref_willow}, decomposition strategies that preserve
cross-window swap interactions, and an extension to the hybrid flow
shop with machine-dependent processing times.
